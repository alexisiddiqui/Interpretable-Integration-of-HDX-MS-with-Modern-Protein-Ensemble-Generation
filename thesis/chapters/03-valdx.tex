\subsection{Experimental data and candidate ensembles}
\label{sec:valdx-inputs}

HDX-MS data require preprocessing before fitting, because peptide redundancy can lead to data leakage during validation, uptake curves at extreme time points (e.g.\ 0~min, saturation) are poorly modelled by single-exponential kinetics, and short peptides provide low information content. Our filtering protocol addresses these limitations before any splitting or optimisation occurs (\cref{app:valdx-hdx-data}).

We obtained published HDX-MS datasets for five protein systems (\cref{tab:valdx-data}). We primarily selected these datasets based on their public availability. We also specifically aimed to cover a broad range of protein sizes and structural properties. Our selection includes rigid proteins, systems with partially complete experimental structures, and highly flexible or sparsely labelled examples such as BRD4.

Regions lacking experimental coverage remain unconstrained by the HDX data and are governed by the prior ensemble and forward model. Strong conclusions about ensemble representativeness should therefore be restricted to measured regions. Gaps in experimental coverage may prevent discrimination between ensembles that differ mainly in unobserved regions.

For BPTI, the primary experimental observables are residue-wise NMR-derived log protection factors rather than peptide-level deuterium-uptake curves.\supercite{leeInterpretingHydrogenDeuteriumExchange2021} To use these data with the HDXer uptake-based objective, we converted each experimental protection factor $P_{f,i}^\text{exp}$ into a residue-wise fractional-uptake curve by substituting it for $P_{f,i}$ in \cref{eq:bg-residue-uptake}, where $k_{\text{int},i}$ is the intrinsic exchange rate for residue $i$ under the specified experimental conditions. Each residue was treated as a separate uptake observation at each modelled time point. These values are therefore NMR-derived residue-wise uptake values, not directly measured peptide-level HDX-MS uptake. Because individual observations correspond to residues rather than overlapping peptides, peptide-overlap leakage does not arise for BPTI. Exchange at distinct residues may nevertheless remain correlated through shared structural environments and conformational changes, making fully independent training and validation sets challenging to obtain. Splits could in principle be designed using residue-pair correlations, although folded proteins may contain too few independently varying regions to support effective statistics. Our Non-Redundant and Spatial strategies (\cref{sec:valdx-splits}) instead partition observations by sequence position and spatial proximity, accounting for two primary routes through which exchange behaviour may be correlated. BPTI therefore tests structured partitioning of correlated residue-wise observations and the distribution of sequence or structural coverage across training and validation sets, but does not test the removal of peptide-overlap leakage in bottom-up HDX-MS. Neither strategy guarantees the absence of correlations between sets.

To test the ValDX framework, we generated a diverse set of structural ensembles for each protein system. These ensembles were designed to represent distinct scientific hypotheses about how to best capture protein dynamics, ranging from single-structure simulations to evolutionary-based predictions and enhanced sampling. Conventional MD ensembles used approximately $100\,\text{ns}$ of simulation per protein, whereas MD-TFES permitted up to $400\,\text{ns}$ and AF2 generation is not directly comparable by simulation time. Comparisons therefore reflect both generation strategy and computational investment within moderate computational budgets. A summary of all generated ensembles is provided in \cref{tab:valdx-ensembles}. The TeaA ensembles used in the synthetic benchmarks are described in \cref{sec:valdx-synthetic}. Extended ensemble characterisation, performance benchmarks and per-protein analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), local cross correlation (LCC) and pairwise ensemble heatmaps for residue-level RMSD and Wasserstein distances, are given in \cref{app:valdx-supporting-results}, and the structural comparisons are defined in \cref{sec:valdx-work}.

The candidate ensembles were generated hierarchically, so they are not independent of one another. AF2-MSAss is the unfiltered set of 12,700 ColabFold predictions obtained by subsampling the multiple sequence alignment (MSA).\supercite{mirditaColabFoldMakingProtein2022,monteirodasilvaHighthroughputPredictionProtein2024} AF2-Filtered is the subset of AF2-MSAss retained after filtering on AF2 confidence scores and structural geometry. Filtering uses only AF2/geometry-derived features and does not use HDX data, preventing circular validation. MD-1Start starts from the single highest-pLDDT structure in the complete AF2-MSAss ensemble (AF2-MaxPLDDT), MD-10Start from ten k-means representatives of AF2-Filtered, and MD-TFES is seeded from both MD ensembles.

The purpose of the various MD experiments performed is to create a range of ensembles with distinct differences in their sampling properties. They are intended to represent broad regimes of approaches to MD and not exhaustive approaches to generating ideal ensembles. The experiments compare distinct ensemble-generation strategies rather than equalised computational budgets.

\subsection{Data splitting and replicate fits}
\label{sec:valdx-splits}

Because peptides share residues, a validation peptide can be partially `known' from training peptides covering similar regions. For example, if residue 50 is measured by five different peptides, information about that residue leaks between training and validation sets, inflating apparent performance. We therefore define splits that minimise shared residue coverage. To test whether an ensemble accurately represents protein dynamics at different structural scales, we designed splitting strategies that isolate distinct types of information.

\paragraph{Non-Redundant split (global scale).}
This split clusters peptides by sequence region, ensuring validation peptides test entirely unseen segments of the protein. This tests the ensemble's ability to predict global conformational behaviour without relying on local overfitting. Specifically, using the start and end positions of each peptide as features, a k-means clustering algorithm is applied using a tenth of the total number of peptides as the number of clusters. Clusters are then randomly assigned according to the specified training fraction; peptides that share at least one residue with a peptide in the other set are then removed from both sets.

\paragraph{Spatial split (local scale).}
This split withholds peptides covering contiguous 3D regions of the protein structure. This tests the ensemble's accuracy in capturing local substructural dynamics, such as domain motions or specific binding site flexibility. A residue is randomly selected from the protein. The remaining residues are ordered by the distance of their $C_{\alpha}$ atoms from the selected residue's $C_{\alpha}$ atom. The training set is filled in order of proximity of peptide-residues. Overlapping peptides are then removed from both sets.

\paragraph{Random and Sequential splits (baselines).}
We also implemented standard random and sequential splits as baselines to demonstrate the insufficiency of methods that do not account for information leakage. These splits typically result in high overlap between training and validation sets. For the random split, peptides are randomly selected for the training set and the remainder used in the validation set. For the sequential split, peptides are ordered based on sequence number and are then selected up to the training fraction; the remainder is used in the validation set.

All splitting algorithms used a target training fraction of 50\%. Removal of overlapping peptides could alter the realised fraction, with both training and validation sets containing at least 10\% of the original peptides. To improve diversity and prevent redundancy-driven bias, peptides with high overlap density were stochastically dropped (10\% dropout rate) prior to splitting. Where splitting fails, overlap can be resolved by adjusting the initial peptide redundancy dropout or directly removing extremely long or redundant peptides. For BPTI the observations are residues rather than peptides, so the same algorithms partition residues (\cref{sec:valdx-inputs}).

ValDX is built around the HDXer optimisation package,\supercite{bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020} but the overall protocol is transferable. The workflow starts with data splitting. For each replicate, ensemble-structural features are computed for both the training and validation observation sets. The training data (peptides for bottom-up HDX-MS or residues for BPTI, together with their structural features) are used for fitting across multiple free-parameter choices ($\gamma_\text{HDXer}$, \cref{sec:valdx-fitting}). Within each split-replicate, representative parameters are selected from the training data using the curve-of-the-knee algorithm, except in the kinetic misspecification benchmark, which uses a normalised selection score (\cref{sec:valdx-synthetic}). From this replicate-parameter set, model parameters and weights are extracted and used to calculate validation MSE as well as Work Done distances (\cref{sec:valdx-work}). These metrics are pooled across replicates and can then be compared in downstream analysis.

Most experiments used three split replicates for each split type, and the BV-parameter optimisation used ten. The Iso-Validation benchmark included all four split types; the reweighting, BV-parameter, clustering and perturbation experiments used the Non-Redundant and Spatial splits. Exact designs are given in \cref{app:valdx-case-studies}. The split replicates are intended to measure sensitivity to peptide-level partitioning, which is distinct from the 5\% RSD filter applied to centroid means at each time point (\cref{app:valdx-hdx-data}).

Between-split agreement complements within-split uncertainty as a diagnostic. Because the two strategies withhold different structural aspects, close agreement between their averages supports a result that does not depend on the partition. Disagreement indicates that the result depends on which structural regions are withheld.

\subsection{Fitting protocols, parameter selection and consensus}
\label{sec:valdx-fitting}

To align simulated ensembles with experimental HDX-MS data, we employ MaxEnt reweighting (\cref{sec:bg-maxent}). This balances experimental agreement against changes to the prior populations. Reweighting cannot add missing conformations or repair implausible structures.

We employed optimisation protocols differing in which parameters were allowed to vary (\cref{tab:valdx-protocols}). BV-only, RW-only and MutualBVRW are standard HDXer protocols. To investigate what happens at each stage of combined approaches, we took the optimised ensembles resulting from BV-only and RW-only and applied the complementary method as a second step, yielding BVafterRW and RWafterBV.

HDXer implements a MaxEnt optimisation framework to reweight the simulated ensemble.\supercite{bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020} Protection factors are predicted from coordinates with the Best--Vendruscolo (BV) model (\cref{eq:bg-bv-feature,eq:bg-bv-average}). The $\beta_C$ and $\beta_H$ scaling factors function purely as empirical calibration parameters rather than mechanistic descriptors. We write $X$ for the prior ensemble-model, its frame weights together with its BV coefficients, and $\hat X$ for the fitted ensemble-model.

We denote HDXer's Apparent Work by $W_\text{app}$, expressing the published formulation (Eq.~6 of Bradshaw et al.\supercite{bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020}) in the notation used here. Apparent Work quantifies the reweighting bias in energy units,
\begin{equation}
    W_\text{app}(X,\hat X) = RT\ln
    \left\langle \exp\left\{
    -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat\lambda_i
    \left[q_i(j)-\langle q_i\rangle_{\hat X}\right]
    \right\}\right\rangle_{\hat X},
    \label{eq:valdx-apparent-work}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes the $N$ modelled residues, $j$ denotes a conformation, $\hat\lambda_i$ are the optimised dimensionless Lagrange multipliers, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\hat X}$ denotes an average over conformations weighted by their probabilities in the reweighted ensemble $\hat X$, so that $\langle q_i\rangle_{\hat X}$ is the ensemble-averaged natural logarithm of the protection factor for residue $i$. Because HDXer weights conformations as $w_j\propto w_j^0\exp[\sum_i\hat\lambda_i q_i(j)]$, \cref{eq:valdx-apparent-work} equals $RT\,D_\text{KL}(w\Vert w^0)$, the molar apparent work of \cref{sec:bg-maxent}. At the 300~K analysis temperature, $RT=2.494$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$.

HDXer finds the multipliers $\hat\lambda_i$ that minimise the regularised objective of \cref{eq:bg-regularised-objective}, in which the KL term is $W_\text{app}/RT$ (keeping the ensemble close to the prior) and the error term measures disagreement with experimental data. In ValDX the error term sums only over training observations,
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_\text{HDXer}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}
    \frac{\left(\hat D_{o,t}^\text{train}(\hat X)-D_{o,t}^\text{train}(Y)\right)^2}{2\eta^2},
    \label{eq:valdx-hdxer-error}
\end{equation}
where $o\in\mathcal O$ indexes an experimental observation (a peptide for the bottom-up HDX-MS datasets and a residue for the BPTI NMR-derived dataset), $t\in\mathcal T$ indexes labelling times, $\hat D_{o,t}^\text{train}(\hat X)$ is the predicted fractional uptake, and $D_{o,t}^\text{train}(Y)$ is the corresponding measured or experimentally derived fractional uptake of the data $Y$. For BPTI, it is the residue-wise uptake calculated from the experimental NMR protection factor (\cref{sec:valdx-inputs}); for the peptide-level datasets, it is the measured peptide fractional uptake. The uncertainty parameter is fixed at $\eta=1$, and $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ is the dimensionless optimisation parameter that balances fitting accuracy against the work required to reweight the ensemble.

Higher $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ values allow more aggressive fitting to experimental data but risk overfitting by forcing large deviations from the prior ensemble. Lower values preserve the prior distribution but may result in poorer fit to experiment. We tested $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ values from $1\times10^0$ to $9\times10^0$, spanning a range from minimal to strong fitting. The exception is the Iso-Validation benchmark, whose grid ran from $1\times10^{-1}$ to $9\times10^{1}$ (\cref{app:valdx-case-iso}); the grid used in each experiment is given in \cref{app:valdx-case-studies}. Larger values of $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ were tested in preliminary analyses but we found that above $\gamma_\text{HDXer}=9\times10^0$, the optimiser would consistently fail in some split-replicates.

\paragraph{Curve-of-the-knee selection.}
The optimal regularisation parameter ($\gamma_\text{HDXer}$) balances fitting accuracy against overfitting. For experiments using knee selection, we identified this balance point automatically by plotting apparent work against training error and finding the ``elbow'', the point where additional fitting strength provides diminishing returns. This was computed as the point where the local gradient approaches $-1$ (45\textdegree\ slope) on the work-error tradeoff curve. The knee was selected from the training error versus apparent work curve (both linear scale), using the $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ grid specified for each experiment in \cref{app:valdx-case-studies}. This is a naive implementation of the qualitative parameter selection method described by HDXer tutorials and literature.\supercite{leeInterpretingHydrogenDeuteriumExchange2021,bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020} The full algorithm is given in \cref{alg:valdx-knee}.

\paragraph{Consensus across replicates.}
For multi-stage protocols, frame weights were mean-averaged and BV parameters median-averaged across replicates to provide the prior for the next stage. Split replicates in staged protocols therefore shared the consensus weights or parameters obtained from the preceding stage. Their variation describes sensitivity to peptide partitioning and does not represent independent measurement-noise replication. Generating a consensus from multiple splits does not inherently produce a representative candidate ensemble. The physical ambiguity of protection factors persists regardless of the partitioning strategy.

\paragraph{Optimisation failures.}
Optimisation runs themselves may fail. Preliminary investigations showed that larger $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ runs are more likely to fail; due to the Lagrangian formulation it is unlikely that all simulations within a replicate fail. If replicates are consistently failing, adding additional replicates can overcome this and reducing $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ leads to more conservative fitting.

\paragraph{Uptake error.}
Training and validation errors ($\text{MSE}_\text{Training}$ and $\text{MSE}_\text{Validation}$) are the mean squared differences between predicted and measured fractional uptake, $\hat D_{o,t}$ and $D_{o,t}$ in the notation of \cref{eq:valdx-hdxer-error}, over the training and withheld validation observations of a split (\cref{eq:bg-validation-error}). To quantify the change in fit to experimental data, we calculate the signed change in mean squared error,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\text{MSE}_\text{opt}=\text{MSE}_\text{final}-\text{MSE}_\text{initial}.
    \label{eq:valdx-delta-mse}
\end{equation}
More negative values indicate a greater reduction in error when fitting the experimental HDX data; zero indicates no change, and positive values indicate increased error following optimisation.

\paragraph{Population recovery.}
In the TeaA benchmarks the target populations are known by construction, so fitted populations can be compared with them directly (\cref{sec:valdx-synthetic}). Fitted open-state populations were calculated by summing the optimised weights assigned to open-state structures and compared with the target population of 0.4. Open State Recovery normalised the fitted open-state population, $p_\text{open}$, to the target,
\begin{equation}
    \text{Open State Recovery}\,(\%)=100\,\frac{p_\text{open}}{0.4}.
    \label{eq:valdx-open-state-recovery}
\end{equation}
Recovery values were not clipped. 100\% indicates recovery of the target open-state population, and values above 100\% indicate its overestimation.

\paragraph{Work Done and Apparent Work.}
Work Done quantifies how much the ensemble-averaged protection-factor profile changes during optimisation. Prior and fitted profiles are each calculated with their own frame weights and BV coefficients, so the comparison includes changes to either set of parameters. Work Done is a distance from the prior and does not measure distance to any ground-truth distribution. It also differs from the Apparent Work reported by HDXer (\cref{eq:valdx-apparent-work}), which describes only the regularised reweighting of frame weights.

These metrics are statistical quantities constructed from protection-factor profiles. Their notation follows a phenomenological analogy in the context of ensemble-model optimisation. Under EX2 conditions, protection factors report apparent opening free energies (\cref{eq:bg-opening-free-energy}), allowing optimisation-induced changes in the predicted protection-factor profile to be expressed in energy units (kJ\,mol$^{-1}$). Uniform energy scaling allows Work Done metrics to be compared across ensembbles and replicates. Smaller values indicate less modification was applied during fitting. Alone, this does not equate to representativeness, as it can indicate lack of fitting.

\paragraph{Work Done components.}
$P_{f,i}$ and $\hat P_{f,i}$ are the protection factors of residue $i$ in the prior and fitted ensemble-models $X$ and $\hat X$. Overbars denote means across the $N$ compared residues. $R$ is the gas constant and $T$ the analysis temperature (300~K, as for $W_\text{app}$). Work metrics are reported in kJ\,mol$^{-1}$. $RT$ in the shape and scale equations is expressed in kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, while $H_\text{opt}$ and $S_\text{opt}$ in the density and total-work equations are in J\,mol$^{-1}$ and J\,mol$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$, respectively, with division by 1000 converting to kJ\,mol$^{-1}$.

Work shape measures how much fitting changes each residue's absolute deviation from the mean log protection factor. We write this deviation as $a_i=|\ln P_{f,i}-\overline{\ln P_f}|$ and define the centred protection score $H_\text{opt}(X;i)=RTa_i$. Deviations above and below the mean contribute identically, so Work shape measures changes in protection contrast and not in its direction.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Work}_\text{shape}
    =\frac{RT}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left|
    \left|\ln P_{f,i}-\overline{\ln P_f}\right|
    -
    \left|\ln\hat P_{f,i}-\overline{\ln\hat P_f}\right|
    \right|
    \label{eq:valdx-work-shape}
\end{equation}

Work scale measures the shift in mean log protection factor during fitting.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Work}_\text{scale}=RT\left|\overline{\ln P_f}-\overline{\ln\hat P_f}\right|
    \label{eq:valdx-work-scale}
\end{equation}

Work density measures the mean absolute change in residue-wise entropy-like contributions across the centred protection profile. Each residue is assigned a weight $w_\text{opt}(X;i)=e^{-a_i}\sum_j e^{-a_j}$, with the sum taken over the compared residues, and a contribution $S_\text{opt}(X;i)=-R\,w_\text{opt}(X;i)\ln w_\text{opt}(X;i)$. Fitted quantities are calculated in the same way from the fitted profile. These weights are indexed over residues. They are not conformational populations, and they are distinct from the frame weights adjusted during reweighting. The weights are not normalised to sum to one, so individual contributions can be negative.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Work}_\text{density}=\frac{T}{1000N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|S_\text{opt}(\hat X;i)-S_\text{opt}(X;i)\right|
    \label{eq:valdx-work-density}
\end{equation}

Total work combines changes in centred protection scores and entropy-like contributions before taking their mean absolute magnitude. Alongside validation error, it measures how much the starting protection profile must change to achieve agreement with experiment.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Work}_\text{opt}
    =\frac{1}{1000N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left|
    \left[H_\text{opt}(\hat X;i)-TS_\text{opt}(\hat X;i)\right]
    -
    \left[H_\text{opt}(X;i)-TS_\text{opt}(X;i)\right]
    \right|
    \label{eq:valdx-work-opt}
\end{equation}

Changes in the two terms can offset within a residue. $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ is therefore at most the sum of $\text{Work}_\text{shape}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{density}$, and it excludes the mean shift reported by $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$. The multiplied weights make Work density and Work opt dependent on the residue set, and division by $N$ does not remove this dependence.

\paragraph{Interpreting the components.}
Successful ensemble validation manifests as low prediction error on withheld peptides ($\text{MSE}_\text{Validation}$) accompanied by small Work Done, indicating that the ensemble matched experimental data without substantial modification. Reproducibility across replicate optimisations and consistency between data partitioning strategies provide additional confidence in the result. The error and Work metrics are read jointly, although no single pattern identifies a unique cause. Low validation error accompanied by high Work Done shows that agreement was reached through substantial modification of the prior. This pattern is consistent with overfitting, but it can also arise from a poor prior or from mismatch in the forward model. The structural hypothesis should therefore be treated with caution, in proportion to the spread of metrics across replicate optimisations. High error together with high Work Done shows that the ensemble could not match the data even after substantial modification.

Large $\text{Work}_\text{shape}$ indicates that optimisation substantially changed which regions appear more or less protected relative to the mean. Possible causes include local structural error, altered hydrogen-bonding patterns and missing conformational states. Elevated $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ indicates a global shift in predicted protection. Possible causes include forward-model calibration, experimental conditions, or a uniform structural difference such as global over- or under-compaction. Because these causes are not separable from the scale term alone, model parameter recalibration should be considered before rejecting the ensemble hypothesis. Changes in $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ in either direction contribute positively, because the metric quantifies departure from the prior rather than assigning thermodynamic favourability to increased or decreased protection. Large values indicate substantial reorganisation of the residue-level protection-factor distribution. Possible causes include prior-ensemble mismatch, missing conformational states, forward-model misspecification, or data-normalisation effects. The metric does not by itself identify which cause is operating.

$\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ provides the primary summary metric for ensemble comparison. Although Work Done constitutes a fitting distance rather than a direct measure of physical correctness, it remains useful as a diagnostic. Together, $\text{Work}_\text{density}$, $\text{Work}_\text{shape}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ help distinguish patterns consistent with several possible failure modes when interpreted jointly with validation error, replicate reproducibility, and controls.

\paragraph{Comparison with Bayesian evidence.}
The HDXer objective (\cref{eq:bg-regularised-objective,eq:valdx-hdxer-error}) can be interpreted as a fixed-error, maximum a posteriori form of Bayesian ensemble refinement. The error density penalises disagreement between predicted and measured or experimentally derived uptake, while the regularisation term preserves proximity to the prior ensemble. This differs from full Bayesian evidence approaches such as BICePs, where nuisance uncertainty parameters are sampled or marginalised and candidate priors are ranked using a partition-function ratio.\supercite{raddiBICePsV20Software2023c} ValDX does not compute this evidence. Instead, it evaluates fitted ensembles using held-out observations and Work Done distances in log protection-factor space. These metrics quantify how much the fitted ensemble differs from the starting hypothesis, rather than the posterior probability of the hypothesis itself.

\paragraph{Work accounting across fitting stages.}
Each Work quantity compares a fitted ensemble-model with a reference prior. For the two-stage protocols, the reference is the prior entering the final stage, which is the consensus of the first stage (\cref{tab:valdx-protocols}). That final stage is reweighting for RWafterBV and BV-parameter optimisation for BVafterRW. Because this reference excludes the first stage, the BPTI protocol comparison additionally reports the total distance from the original unoptimised ensemble for these two-step protocols, designated BVafterRW* and RWafterBV*. The kinetic-misspecification benchmark reports the final stage only, in the unstarred notation, and takes $W_\text{app}$ from the final-stage \texttt{work.dat} output at the selected $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$. The separately calculated frame-weight KL divergence uses uniform reference weights, so BVafterRW reflects population changes inherited from the preceding reweighting stage. This divergence is expressed in nats. These quantities describe statistical changes rather than a decomposition of physical potential energy.

\paragraph{Normalisation against the parent ensemble.}
To quantify the effect of structural artefacts relative to the unmodified parent ensemble, we express each metric as a Z-score,
% Follows projects/1-ValDX/analyses@1d78b96: notebooks/Figure_5_Poisoned_Ensemble/BRD4_Compare_Poisons.ipynb (cells 44--45).
\begin{equation}
    Z_m=\frac{m-\mu_{m,0}}{\sigma_m},
    \label{eq:valdx-z-score}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the metric value for one split replicate, $\mu_{m,0}$ is its mean over the split replicates of the corresponding unmodified parent ensemble (0\% poisoned, \cref{sec:valdx-decoys}), and $\sigma_m$ is its sample standard deviation (divisor $n-1$) over the split replicates at all doses (0--50\%) for the same ensemble and perturbation type. We apply this normalisation to $\Delta\text{MSE}_\text{opt}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ under the Non-Redundant split. For each ensemble and perturbation type, the dose response is summarised by the population standard deviation ($SD$) of the mean Z-scores at 0, 0.1, 1 and 10\% and by their Pearson correlation ($R$) with the number of added structures (0, 1, 10 and 100). The 50\% dose is excluded from these summaries but contributes to $\sigma_m$. Because $\sigma_m$ is specific to each ensemble and perturbation type, $SD$ describes response spread in that ensemble's own normalised units rather than absolute sensitivity in the original metric units.

\paragraph{Split-specific optimisation reproducibility.}
For each split type $s\in\{\mathrm{NR},\mathrm{Sp}\}$, we represented replicate $r$ by $\mathbf u_{s,r}=(\text{Work}_{\text{scale},s,r},\text{Work}_{\text{density},s,r})^\top$ and quantified replicate-to-replicate dispersion as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_s=\left[\frac{1}{n_\text{rep}-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n_\text{rep}}\left\lVert\mathbf u_{s,r}-\bar{\mathbf u}_s\right\rVert_2^2\right]^{1/2}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\left(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf u_s)\right)},
    \label{eq:valdx-replicate-dispersion}
\end{equation}
where $n_\text{rep}$ is the number of replicate partitions and $\bar{\mathbf u}_s$ is their centroid. Because both Work components are reported in kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, $\sigma_s$ has units of kJ\,mol$^{-1}$. Lower $\sigma_s$ indicates that optimisation outcomes are more reproducible across replicate partitions; we refer to this operationally as higher confidence.

{\emergencystretch=1em Throughout, ``global confidence'' denotes reproducibility under the Non-Redundant split ($\sigma_\mathrm{NR}$), which withholds non-overlapping sequence regions, whereas ``local confidence'' denotes reproducibility under the Spatial split ($\sigma_\mathrm{Sp}$), which withholds spatially contiguous regions. These terms are shorthand for split-specific optimisation reproducibility, not calibrated probabilities, confidence intervals, or independent evidence of global or local structural accuracy.\par}

\paragraph{Agreement between split types.}
To assess agreement between split types, we compare their representative Work pairs. For each split type $s$, let $\mathbf u_s^{(\mathrm{mean})}=\bar{\mathbf u}_s$ and let $\mathbf u_s^{(\mathrm{median})}$ contain the component-wise medians of $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ across replicate partitions. We define split agreement as
% Thesis formalisation of projects/1-ValDX/manuscript/report/7_results_omc.tex L121.
% The manuscript gives no equation. The 1/2 normalisation follows the plotting code in
% projects/1-ValDX/analyses@1d78b96: notebooks/Figure-2_Single_Split_BV/Figure2_HOIP.ipynb (cell 162)
% and notebooks/Figure-2.5_RW_Splits_Compare_Experiments/BPTI_Compare_RW_ensembles.ipynb (cell 129).
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSD}_q
    =\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\left(u_{\mathrm{NR},k}^{(q)}-u_{\mathrm{Sp},k}^{(q)}\right)^2\right]^{1/2}
    =\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf u_\mathrm{NR}^{(q)}-\mathbf u_\mathrm{Sp}^{(q)}\right\rVert_2}{\sqrt{2}},
    \qquad q\in\{\mathrm{mean},\mathrm{median}\}.
    \label{eq:valdx-split-agreement}
\end{equation}
The factor of $1/2$ averages the squared differences over the two Work components, which enter in kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ without standardisation. Mean and median RMSD therefore retain these units, with lower values indicating closer agreement between split summaries. This comparison does not require pairing replicates across split types and complements the within-split dispersion $\sigma_s$.

\paragraph{Structural comparison.}
To evaluate whether generated ensembles capture biologically relevant dynamics, we employed a suite of structural analysis metrics that probe protein behaviour at different scales (\cref{sec:bg-ensemble-comparison}). These metrics allow us to quantify (1) the global diversity of sampled conformations, (2) the similarity between different ensemble generation protocols, and (3) the local flexibility and correlated motions of specific residue networks. To visualise the high-dimensional conformational landscape covered by each ensemble, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the pairwise C$\alpha$ distances of all structures; residues were filtered by HDX-MS peptide coverage. This projection reveals the major modes of structural variation and allows for comparison of sampling coverage, without needing a reference. For clustering and representative structure selection, we used k-means clustering on the first 10 principal components.

To compare local mean geometry between ensembles, we calculated the RMS difference between ensemble-mean C$\alpha$--C$\alpha$ distances within residue-centred neighbourhoods. Each neighbourhood was defined from the first ensemble's mean coordinates and held fixed for the comparison. This unweighted measure compares mean pair distances and does not characterise the complete ensemble distributions. To quantify how different two ensembles appear at each residue position, we computed the Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_1$). Unlike the local mean pair-distance RMS difference, which compares mean geometry, $W_1$ compares, for each residue, the distributions of its C$\alpha$ distances to all C$\alpha$ atoms, pooled over frames, and so accounts for the spread of those distances as well as their mean. Values are averaged over residues. Agreement of these per-residue pair-distance distributions does not establish that two ensembles share the same joint conformational distribution.

To measure the local flexibility of the protein chain, we computed the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), weighted by structure probabilities. This metric quantifies how much each residue moves around its average position, allowing us to identify rigid domains versus flexible loops. We assessed correlated residue displacements separately within each ensemble using dynamical local cross correlation (LCC). For each neighbouring residue pair, we calculated the normalised covariance of their mean-centred C$\alpha$ displacement vectors over frames, then averaged the valid pair correlations over each residue's neighbours. Positive values indicate correlated displacements, negative values indicate anti-correlated displacements, and values near zero indicate weak net correlation. Averaging over neighbours can also produce values near zero when positive and negative pair correlations cancel. Dynamical LCC describes displacement correlations within an ensemble, whereas the structural local-distance correlation used during AF2 cleaning (\cref{eq:valdx-structural-ldc}) compares distance patterns between individual structures. Definitions of the pair-distance RMS difference, $W_1$, RMSF and LCC are given in \cref{app:valdx-structural-metrics}.

\subsection{Benchmark designs and statistical analysis}
\label{sec:valdx-benchmarks}

\subsubsection{Synthetic population and kinetic benchmarks}
\label{sec:valdx-synthetic}

To establish controlled conditions enabling direct accuracy measurement, we generated synthetic HDX-uptake data with known conformational populations. The synthetic benchmarks use TeaA, a domain of the membrane transporter complex TeaABC that undergoes large-scale conformational transitions between open and closed states during substrate transport.\supercite{marinelliEvidenceAllostericMechanism2011,leeInterpretingHydrogenDeuteriumExchange2021} The TeaA trajectory used for the Iso-Validation experiment originates from the bias-exchange metadynamics simulations reported by Marinelli et al.\supercite{marinelliEvidenceAllostericMechanism2011} We used the unbiased replica representing equilibrium dynamics, accessed through the HDXer tutorial/data materials described by Bradshaw et al., which we cite for trajectory processing and HDXer methods.\supercite{bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020}

Frames were clustered into open and closed states by their RMSD from reference structures (\cref{app:valdx-teaa-states}). The ISO-Bimodal ensemble is formed by taking the structures directly from the clustering process. Its native open to closed ratio is 5:95. We reweighted it to 40:60 open to closed and predicted uptake from the reweighted populations, giving the synthetic target used for fitting. The ISO-Trimodal ensemble is the parent ensemble of the clustered, ISO-Bimodal ensemble. ISO-Bimodal contains exclusively ground-truth structures present in the synthetic data, so its target populations can in principle be recovered by reweighting. ISO-Trimodal additionally includes intermediate conformations absent from the target.

Synthetic protection factors were computed using the BV model with a smooth switching function. The switching function raises and smoothes the protection factors for a given structure. All optimisation in this study instead used the hard-cutoff BV model defined in \cref{eq:bg-bv-feature}. This introduces a forward-model mismatch between target generation and reweighting, which applies equally to both candidate ensembles. Synthetic HDX-uptake curves were generated without added measurement noise to isolate the effects of ensemble composition from experimental uncertainty.

To extend the staged BV-model optimisation benchmark, we tested how fitting protocols respond to errors in the assumed exchange kinetics. The kinetic regime was imposed and known, and we supplied an ensemble with ground-truth structures only. We used the TeaA ISO-Bimodal ensemble, which contains both conformational states contributing to the synthetic target. The target populations were fixed at 40\% open and 60\% closed, while the starting ensemble retained its original population imbalance. Across kinetic conditions, the structural ensemble and target populations were unchanged, while the opening and closing timescales used to generate uptake were varied. Residue protection factors were calculated from the target ensemble using the hard-cutoff BV model with $\beta_C=0.35$ and $\beta_H=2.0$. Unlike the earlier Iso-Validation experiment, the same structural protection-factor model was used for target generation and fitting.

Uptake was generated using the residue-level form of the scheme in \cref{eq:bg-ll-scheme}, $C_i\rightleftharpoons O_i\rightarrow D_i$, where $C_i$ and $O_i$ are unexchanged closed and open states and $D_i$ is the exchanged state. For each residue, rates were defined as
\begin{equation}
    k_{\text{cl},i}=\rho\,k_{\text{int},i},
    \qquad
    k_{\text{op},i}=\frac{k_{\text{cl},i}}{P_{f,i}-1}.
    \label{eq:valdx-kinetic-rates}
\end{equation}
Here, $\rho$ controls the closing timescale relative to intrinsic exchange while preserving the equilibrium open probability, $1/P_{f,i}$. These residue-level opening probabilities are distinct from the global TeaA open-state population. Although the same $\rho$ is applied to every residue, its effect on uptake depends on the residue-specific intrinsic exchange rate, $k_{\text{int},i}$, and protection factor, $P_{f,i}$. Varying $\rho$ therefore changes uptake magnitudes and time-course shapes rather than applying a uniform multiplicative uptake correction. The experiment tests kinetic misspecification while preserving the generating equilibrium populations. Each residue was initialised at equilibrium, and uptake was calculated by matrix exponentiation of the kinetic rate equations (\cref{app:valdx-kinetic-simulator}). We validated this simulator with three checks, detailed in \cref{app:valdx-kinetic-simulator}. These checks assess the numerical implementation and its agreement with the EX2 limit; they do not establish recovery of the generating BV parameters or conformational populations through fitting.

A sequence-based stochastic pepsin digestion model generated 94 overlapping peptides independently of kinetic condition (\cref{app:valdx-peptide-noise}). Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.02 in fractional uptake was added using matched noise realisations across kinetic conditions. Noisy values were clipped to the interval $[0,1]$. The fraction of noisy values clipped to $[0,1]$ rose with kinetic misspecification, from 2.55\% of values at $\rho=1000$ to 3.83\% at $\rho=1$ and 14.89\% at $\rho=0.01$. The comparisons therefore reflect both kinetic misspecification and the associated differences in boundary clipping; these experiments do not separately quantify their contributions to fitting performance.

All datasets were fitted using the standard EX2/BV forward model without supplying the generating kinetic regime. Comparisons were paired across kinetic conditions using the $\rho=1000$ condition as the EX2-like reference. To account for the different scales of training error and Apparent Work across kinetic conditions, model selection used a normalised score. For each kinetic condition, protocol and split replicate, training MSE ($E_\gamma$) and Apparent Work ($W_\gamma$, \cref{eq:valdx-apparent-work}) were independently min--max-normalised across the candidate values in that reweighting stage's $\gamma$ sweep,
\begin{equation}
    s_\gamma = \frac{E_\gamma-E_{\min}}{E_{\max}-E_{\min}}
             + \frac{W_\gamma-W_{\min}}{W_{\max}-W_{\min}}.
    \label{eq:valdx-kinetic-selection-score}
\end{equation}
The selected $\gamma$ minimised $s_\gamma$, giving equal weight to the two normalised terms; ties were resolved by selecting the lower $\gamma$. The minima and maxima were calculated separately within each split's sweep, without pooling across kinetic conditions or replicates. Validation MSE and population recovery were not used for selection. Reported MSE and Work values retain their original scales.

Absolute population estimates and changes relative to the EX2-like reference were evaluated separately to distinguish baseline recovery limitations from additional population distortion caused by kinetic misspecification.

\subsubsection{Clustering and structural perturbations}
\label{sec:valdx-decoys}

Clustering reduces each parent ensemble to a smaller set of representative structures, which enter fitting with uniform prior weights. We tested whether ensemble comparisons changed when fitting used fewer representative structures. Parent ensembles of 10,000 to 12,700 structures were clustered by k-means on the first 10 principal components (\cref{sec:valdx-work}) to 0.1, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100\% of their size, and each clustered ensemble was reweighted (\cref{app:valdx-case-clustering}).

Structure poisoning experiments were designed to evaluate how ensemble optimisation responds to controlled structural perturbations. These experiments serve as negative controls to assess whether optimisation algorithms can distinguish between physically plausible and artificially corrupted conformations. Two poisoning strategies were implemented to test different aspects of structural integrity, alongside an unmodified control that adds copies of existing structures (\cref{tab:valdx-poisoning}). Detailed algorithms and perturbation procedures are given in \cref{app:valdx-perturbations}.

\paragraph{Gaussian noise.}
{\emergencystretch=1em Gaussian noise poisoning adds residue-specific random perturbations to atomic coordinates, testing sensitivity to disrupted local geometry. The algorithm calculates residue-specific variance from the parent ensemble, then applies normally distributed coordinate perturbations scaled to match the observed fluctuations. This ties the perturbation scale to the parent ensemble, but the uncorrelated displacements do not preserve physically realistic molecular geometry and therefore do not reproduce unfolded or flexible states. In terms of protection factors, this has the effect of reducing the scale of computed backbone potentials.\par}

\paragraph{Coordinate mixing.}
Coordinate mixing represents a conceptually more severe structural perturbation by randomly swapping atomic positions within each frame. At the protection factor level, this preserves global structural density (HDX-uptake scale) but effectively smooths the protection factors. By testing ensembles against protection factor blurring, this should minimise scale differences of poisoned structures and so more directly probes the physicality of ensembles.

\paragraph{Unmodified control.}
The third operation was designed to shuffle backbone amide hydrogen positions at fixed heavy-atom geometry, but its atom selection matched no atoms and left every frame unchanged (\cref{app:valdx-perturbations}). Each dose therefore added copies of structures already in the unmodified ensemble. This control introduces no new conformations and provides a baseline for the responses to corrupted structures.

The poisoning experiments used a separate clustering of each unmodified ensemble to 1000 structures. Poisoned or copied structures were added to it sequentially, so each larger dose contains the structures added at the smaller doses (\cref{app:valdx-poison-construction}). For the two artefacts, the 50\% dose served as a negative control.

\subsubsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:valdx-statistics}

Candidate ensembles and protocols were compared by jointly assessing validation error, Work Done distances from their priors, replicate variability, and agreement across data splits. Reported p-values were unadjusted, and exact values are given in the text and in \cref{app:valdx-supporting-results}. Independent between-ensemble comparisons used Student's two-sample t-tests for TeaA and Welch's two-sample t-tests for BPTI and HOIP. One-sample t-tests assessed changes against zero within each ensemble or protocol, as specified in \cref{app:valdx-supporting-results}. Plot annotations mark the thresholds $\dagger$ $p<0.25$, $\ddagger$ $p<0.1$, * $p<0.05$, ** $p<0.01$, *** $p<0.001$ and **** $p<0.0001$. The two looser thresholds are marked because the reproducibility of bottom-up HDX-MS ranges from 6.5\% to 22.3\% across labelling conditions, with a weighted mean of 9.0\%.\supercite{hudgensInterlaboratoryComparisonHydrogenDeuterium2019} Differences of this size can be masked by experimental variability, so weaker between-ensemble differences are marked for context rather than discarded. Analyses used the \texttt{scipy.stats} and \texttt{statsmodels} Python packages.

We also assessed associations with population recovery and sensitivity to split type using multiple linear regression, incremental $\Delta R^2$, one-way ANOVA and $\eta^2$ (\cref{app:valdx-statistics}). Regression coefficients describe conditional associations. Because the predictors co-vary along the shared regularisation path, their magnitudes should not be used to rank predictor importance. Incremental $\Delta R^2$ measures the additional fraction of total outcome variance explained within the fitted model; it does not establish predictive performance on unseen data. The kinetic-misspecification benchmark was analysed descriptively.

Traditional assessments of ensemble-data integration rely on minimising prediction errors, which quantify how well computed HDX-uptake curves match experimental measurements. We investigated whether this error-centric assessment adequately captures integration success, particularly when multiple structural hypotheses yield similar experimental agreement. To address this question with a known baseline (ground truth), we used the TeaA Iso-Validation benchmark, in which the target populations are known by construction (\cref{sec:valdx-synthetic,fig:valdx-iso-validation}). Its open and closed states are structurally distinct, which makes TeaA a suitable system for testing whether current metrics can identify ensemble representativeness after reweighting.\supercite{leeInterpretingHydrogenDeuteriumExchange2021}

We fitted two structural hypotheses to the same synthetic target. ISO-Bimodal, the clustered ensemble, represents only the two functional end-states. ISO-Trimodal, its unclustered parent, also includes intermediate conformations (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-validation}B--C). Following standard practice in MaxEnt reweighting, we performed ensemble reweighting across a range of $\gamma_\text{HDXer}$ values.\supercite{hummerBayesianEnsembleRefinement2015,kofingerEfficientEnsembleRefinement2019,cesariUsingMaximumEntropy2018,leeInterpretingHydrogenDeuteriumExchange2021,kihnModelingNativeEnsemble2021} The experimental design employed four split types with three replicates each. Random and Sequential splits represent standard approaches, alongside our Non-Redundant and Spatial splits that account for peptide overlap (\cref{app:valdx-case-iso}).

Training error failed to distinguish between ensembles despite their different structural content. Both ensembles achieved similarly low $\text{MSE}_\text{Training}$ values. The variation in training error between different split types of the \textit{same} ensemble exceeded the variation between \textit{different} ensembles using identical splits (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-validation}D--E). Split-replicates maintain consistent peptide distributions across both ensemble experiments. This pattern indicates that optimisation is highly dependent on which specific peptides are used for training. Peptide sampling artefacts therefore dominate over genuine structural differences in training error.

We tested training error directly against ground truth by comparing $\text{MSE}_\text{Training}$ with Open State Recovery, which measures integration success independently of uptake (\cref{eq:valdx-open-state-recovery}). For ISO-Bimodal (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-validation}F), lower training error did correlate with improved ground-truth recovery, reaching a performance plateau between 80\% and 90\% across all split types. ISO-Bimodal, which contains only reference structures, could be reweighted towards the target populations when guided by uptake error alone. However, for ISO-Trimodal (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-validation}G), the relationship collapsed. Open State Recovery struggled to exceed 40\% for three of four split types despite achieving low training error comparable to ISO-Bimodal. The intermediate structures are physically plausible conformations, but they carry no population in the synthetic target. Their presence prevented recovery even though uptake matched the target. Because the target populations are known, this shows that low uptake error alone does not establish correct fitted populations.

Given the limitations of error-based metrics, we investigated whether Apparent Work, which quantifies the modification of frame weights during reweighting (\cref{eq:valdx-apparent-work}), might better assess integration quality. We compared ground-truth Open State Recovery against Apparent Work, computed by HDXer from the fit to training data. For both ensembles, increasing Apparent Work consistently indicated improved Open State Recovery (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-validation}H--I). Within a single ensemble, greater Apparent Work reflects correction of biased starting populations as fitting strength increases. When comparing candidate ensembles at comparable predictive agreement, smaller Work Done instead indicates that the starting ensemble required less correction. Apparent Work distributions showed greater consistency across split types than $\text{MSE}_\text{Training}$ distributions (\cref{fig:valdx-iso-proxy-recovery}). Sensitivity to peptide selection therefore warrants consideration when comparing these metrics.

To assess the additional association of each metric with population recovery, we fitted a multiple linear regression model with standardised predictors relating $\text{MSE}_\text{Training}$ and Apparent Work to ground-truth Open State Recovery (\cref{eq:valdx-recovery-regression,fig:valdx-iso-split-sensitivity}A). Incremental $\Delta R^2$ (\cref{eq:valdx-delta-r2}) was $0.066$ for Apparent Work and $0.040$ for training MSE in ISO-Bimodal. In ISO-Trimodal, the corresponding values were $0.129$ and approximately zero. Apparent Work thus provided a modest additional contribution to explained variance, with the clearest contrast against the near-zero training-MSE contribution in ISO-Trimodal.

Split type explained $13.2$--$14.7$\% of training-MSE variance ($\eta^2=0.132$--$0.147$; $F(3,230)=11.7$--$13.2$, $p=5.23\times10^{-8}$--$3.68\times10^{-7}$), compared with $0.0$--$0.3$\% for Apparent Work ($\eta^2=0.000$--$0.003$; $F(3,230)=0.02$--$0.24$, $p=0.871$--$0.995$; \cref{fig:valdx-iso-split-sensitivity}B--C). These results indicate greater sensitivity to split choice for training MSE.

Together, these analyses support Apparent Work as a complementary indicator of population recovery with lower observed split sensitivity in this benchmark. Additional comparisons of validation error, Work shape, and Work density are shown in \cref{fig:valdx-iso-work-metrics}.

Where target populations are unknown, validation error ($\text{MSE}_\text{Validation}$) becomes our only available proxy for assessing generalisation to unseen data. However, when applied to experimental peptide datasets, Random and Sequential splits provided no additional discriminatory power beyond training error. Consequently, the remaining experiments in this chapter employ exclusively Non-Redundant and Spatial split types, which account for peptide redundancy and provide complementary structural perspectives.

\section{Comparing structural hypotheses}
\label{sec:valdx-structural-hypotheses}

\subsection{BPTI: comparing ensembles through reweighting}
\label{sec:valdx-bpti-reweighting}

\begin{figure}[htbp]

P-values in panels C, E and F are from Welch's between-ensemble tests; panel D uses one-sample tests against zero (\cref{sec:valdx-statistics,tab:valdx-bpti-tests}). Having established that training error alone is insufficient to evaluate ensemble quality, we applied the ValDX framework to experimental HDX data to test whether Work Done metrics could distinguish between structurally similar yet biologically distinct ensemble proposals. We chose Bovine Pancreatic Trypsin Inhibitor (BPTI), a well-characterised small protein commonly used for method validation whose conformational dynamics have been extensively studied.\supercite{mousaBPTIFoldingRevisited2018,zakharovaFunctionalStructuralRoles2008,qinProteinFoldingGuides2015,shawAtomiclevelCharacterizationStructural2010} This protein's rigid core and flexible hairpin region make it a suitable test case for evaluating whether our metrics can detect subtle conformational differences that arise from different ensemble generation approaches. Its observations are residue-wise uptake values derived from NMR protection factors (\cref{sec:valdx-inputs}). This case study therefore assesses ensemble representativeness under partitions of correlated residue-wise observations but does not test the removal of peptide-overlap leakage.

We compared two ensemble generation methods that sample BPTI conformational space differently. MD-1Start represents conventional equilibrium molecular dynamics initiated from a single high-confidence AF2 structure. AF2-Filtered contains diverse conformations generated using AlphaFold2 with MSA subsampling, filtered to remove physically implausible geometries (\cref{tab:valdx-ensembles}). This comparison directly tests whether statistical approaches, which sample global conformational changes through evolutionary variation rather than local physical dynamics, can capture relevant solution-state protein behaviour when compared to traditional simulation methods for BPTI. Both ensembles underwent identical RW-only fitting, which reweights ensemble populations to align predicted uptake with the residue-wise uptake curves without modifying the structures themselves. We used three replicate data partitions for both Non-Redundant splits (testing global consistency across measured sequence regions) and Spatial splits (testing local consistency by withholding spatially contiguous groups of observed residues). Ensemble generation and fitting protocols are detailed in \cref{app:valdx-case-rw,app:valdx-af2,app:valdx-md-protocols}.

The two ensembles exhibit meaningful structural differences despite sampling the same protein within the computational budgets used here. To quantify these differences, we computed the residue Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_{1,i}$, \cref{eq:valdx-w1}) between the two ensembles' pooled C$\alpha$ distance distributions (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}A). The largest structural divergence appeared at BPTI's flexible N- and C-termini ($W_{1,i}=0.5$~\AA) and at the disulphide bridge-containing outer loop of the hairpin turn. This hairpin region is particularly informative as localised conformational diversity here correlates with global conformational changes relevant to BPTI's solution behaviour.\supercite{zakharovaFunctionalStructuralRoles2008,qinProteinFoldingGuides2015} While 20~ns per replicate (100~ns total) is not sufficient to obtain conformational coverage in MD-1Start, even publicly available 1~ms MD simulations show incomplete sampling of this region, relative to predictions from experimental studies.\supercite{shawAtomiclevelCharacterizationStructural2010} Complete coverage of the \textit{in vitro} disulphide torsion angles is difficult to achieve through simulation. We address this by generating ensembles with multiple starting conformations or topologies (MD-10Start and MD-TFES).

In principal component space, AF2-Filtered forms a superset of MD-1Start (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}B, \cref{fig:valdx-pca-bpti}). It shares the primary conformational basin while additionally sampling a minor well corresponding to opening of the outer-hairpin turn. Do rare, hairpin-opening states exist in solution, or does AlphaFold2's sampling strategy introduce conformational heterogeneity essentially absent in solution? Answering this question requires methods that go beyond structural comparison alone.

Validation error alone did not resolve the ensemble ranking. MD-1Start and AF2-Filtered showed similar validation errors in both data partitions (Welch's t-tests, $p=0.765$--$0.975$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}C). Both ensembles showed modest error reductions after reweighting; neither ensemble's mean change differed from zero in either split ($p=0.170$--$0.824$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}D). We therefore examined the protection-factor modifications required to achieve these fits.

$\text{Work}_\text{shape}$, which captures how much optimisation redistributed relative protection across residues (\cref{eq:valdx-work-shape}), was lower for AF2-Filtered than for MD-1Start in the Spatial split ($p=0.0517$) and similar between ensembles in the Non-Redundant split ($p=0.249$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}E). To distinguish relative protection-factor pattern changes from distributional reorganisation, we examined $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ (\cref{eq:valdx-work-density}). This metric was also lower for AF2-Filtered in the Spatial split ($p=0.000678$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}F) and similar between ensembles in the Non-Redundant split ($p=0.150$).

MD-1Start had lower mean $\text{Work}_\text{shape}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ in Non-Redundant fits, but neither metric separated the ensembles there. These Work Done analyses revealed differences in fitting requirements beyond the global structural comparisons, with the clearest separation under local partitions.

To place these findings in broader context, we compared MD-1Start and AF2-Filtered against the full panel of ensemble generation approaches (\cref{tab:valdx-ensembles}) by plotting validation error change ($\Delta\text{MSE}_\text{opt}$) against total Work Done ($\text{Work}_\text{opt}$, \cref{eq:valdx-work-opt}, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}G). Because all five ensembles were fitted with the same protocol, the comparison is not confounded by protocol-specific optimisation choices. MD-1Start and AF2-Filtered were our most representative BPTI ensembles, requiring the smallest modifications upon optimisation (particularly evident in Spatial splits, marked by crosses). In Non-Redundant splits (circles), AF2-Filtered's total Work Done approached that of MD-10Start, with overlapping replicate values suggesting comparable global representativeness. These three ensembles separated from two less representative proposals, MD-TFES and the AF2-MSAss ensemble prior to geometric filtering.

The relative positioning of these ensembles does not separate genuine population shifts from structural implausibility. To distinguish these scenarios, we quantified optimisation uncertainty across replicate fits, computing the multivariate standard deviation ($\sigma_s$, \cref{eq:valdx-replicate-dispersion}) of $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ across the three split replicates (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}). Low multivariate spread indicates the optimiser converges reproducibly regardless of which specific residues comprise the BPTI training versus validation sets, suggesting the ensemble contains primarily valid structures that can be consistently reweighted. High spread indicates fitting instability as different residue selections drive the optimiser towards incompatible solutions. This suggests structural implausibility or severe population imbalance in the candidate ensemble.

MD-1Start proved both representative (requiring small Work Done) and confident (achieving reproducible fits) at both structural scales ($\sigma=0.03$ for both splits, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}C). Equilibrium MD from a high-confidence AF2 structure can therefore provide a reproducible and reliable representation of BPTI's dominant conformational ensemble, although it does not achieve complete coverage of rare hairpin-opening states. In contrast, AF2-Filtered showed modestly improved confidence in Spatial splits ($\sigma=0.02$) but much lower confidence in Non-Redundant splits ($\sigma=0.40$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}B), comparable to MD-10Start's Non-Redundant performance ($\sigma=0.49$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}D). MD-1Start produces equally reproducible fits under both split types, whereas AF2-Filtered fitting is reproducible primarily for sub-structural regions.

Despite lower global reproducibility, MD-10Start showed similar $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ to AF2-Filtered across most replicates in Non-Redundant splits (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}D versus \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}B). Both ensembles therefore require comparable magnitude adjustments to fit global HDX patterns, despite AF2-Filtered's lower Spatial-split $\text{Work}_\text{density}$. This indicates a global over- or under-prediction of exchange rates even when local conformational heterogeneity is reasonably captured. MD-10Start's overlapping behaviour between Non-Redundant and Spatial splits in both $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ space is consistent with a skewed conformational density estimate, in which diverse and individually plausible conformations require rebalancing.

Conversely, MD-1Start required minimal $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ in both splits yet showed larger $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ in Spatial than in Non-Redundant splits. Its predicted exchange magnitudes therefore match experimental observations at all scales, while local conformational differences still demand reorganisation of protection factors to capture regional heterogeneity.

MD-TFES showed that large protection-factor reorganisation requirements ($\text{Work}_\text{density}$) do not necessarily indicate structural implausibility when accompanied by reasonable fitting confidence. MD-TFES maintained better reproducibility than MD-10Start ($\sigma=0.23$--$0.38$ versus $0.31$--$0.49$, \cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}E), with $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ approaching, but remaining distinctly lower than, the unfiltered AF2-MSAss ensemble (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}A, $\sigma=1.30$--$1.35$). MD-TFES represents our broadest coverage of geometrically valid structures, and thus our most deliberately non-equilibrium density estimate for a small, relatively rigid protein like BPTI. This comparison separated valid but unnaturally weighted conformational diversity (MD-TFES, which maintained fitting stability) from an ensemble with known geometric violations (unfiltered AF2-MSAss, which showed both extreme Work Done and fitting instability). $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ magnitude and replicate reproducibility therefore provide complementary information. Here, large reorganisation with reproducible fitting accompanied sampling bias, whereas irreproducible fitting accompanied geometric violations. Neither pattern identifies its cause without reference ensembles such as these.

Comparing the candidates jointly across partitions, Work Done metrics and replicate dispersion ranked and differentiated them as an integrated set rather than in isolation, without requiring detailed prior system knowledge or direct pairwise ensemble comparisons. BPTI's extensive literature characterisation allowed this ranking to be checked retrospectively. Most experimental systems lack such a reference. Where none exists, we recommend this complementary assessment when comparing structural hypotheses derived from different generation methods, particularly when validation metrics based solely on predicted-experimental agreement fail to discriminate between plausible alternatives.

\subsection{HOIP: comparing ensembles and individual structures through BV parameter fitting}
\label{sec:valdx-hoip-parameter-fitting}

Having established our framework on BPTI using structural reweighting, we investigated whether individual structures could be evaluated using model parameter optimisation alone, without the computationally expensive ensemble reweighting employed previously. We hypothesised that BV-parameter fitting, which adjusts how residue-specific contact and hydrogen-bond counts contribute to predicted protection factors without modifying frame weights, would exhibit increased variance in the modifications required during optimisation. To test this hypothesis, we optimised the BV parameters from their default values, as detailed in \cref{eq:bg-bv-feature,eq:bg-bv-average}. Since model parameter optimisation requires considerably less computational expense than structural reweighting (approximately one-third of computation time spent on structural feature calculation), we performed 10 replicates for both data splits to account for this reduced complexity. Because model parameter fitting operates at the ensemble-average level without modifying individual frame weights, we could evaluate not only complete ensembles but also individual structures in isolation. Experiment details can be found in \cref{app:valdx-case-bv}.

For this investigation we selected the flexible E3 ubiquitin ligase HOIL-1L Interacting Protein (HOIP),\supercite{tsaiSingleDomainAntibodiesCrystallization2020} a system where conformational flexibility is essential to catalytic function. The uncertain populations of its conformations under solution conditions made HOIP a suitable test case for our evaluation framework. We directly compared two structures (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}A). AF2-MaxRank is the top-ranked structure from ColabFold using default MSA settings, ranked by predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT). AF2-MaxPLDDT is the structure with the highest pLDDT from the entire AF2-MSAss ensemble. Both structures are physically plausible and could exist on HOIP's conformational landscape. AF2-MaxRank adopts an extended conformation that matches the crystal structure obtained using nanobodies as crystallisation co-factors (PDB 6SC6).\supercite{tsaiSingleDomainAntibodiesCrystallization2020} This is likely a crystallographically stabilised conformation, and its population under solution conditions is uncertain. In contrast, AF2-MaxPLDDT adopts a more compact conformation and has higher pLDDT, making it the more confident and physically plausible candidate. The known flexibility of HOIP nevertheless suggests that this structure alone cannot fully represent the \textit{in vitro} dynamics.

We first examined validation error to assess whether traditional metrics could distinguish between these structures. Although the solution population of the extended AF2-MaxRank conformation is uncertain, validation error ($\text{MSE}_\text{Validation}$, \cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}B) showed no difference between structures (Welch's t-tests, $p=0.267$--$0.530$), consistent with our observations for BPTI (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}C). However, when we examined error change upon optimisation ($\Delta\text{MSE}_\text{opt}$, \cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}C), the difference between structures had smaller p-values than for validation error alone (one-sample t-tests against zero, $p=0.000185$--$0.0103$). Compared to structural reweighting with BPTI, the scale of $\Delta\text{MSE}_\text{opt}$ with model parameter optimisation on HOIP was two orders of magnitude larger, which we attribute to the lack of regularisation in model parameter optimisation within HDXer.

We then examined our Work Done metrics to probe structural quality more deeply. For protection-factor pattern redistribution ($\text{Work}_\text{shape}$, \cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}D), we observed increased values for both splits relative to the BPTI case, with the Non-Redundant split showing extremely large values in some replicates. This variability across replicates indicated a lack of certainty in the fit for both structures, exacerbated by the unconstrained nature of model parameter optimisation.

For protection factor reorganisation requirements ($\text{Work}_\text{density}$, \cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}E), we observed that both structures behaved similarly in the Spatial split ($p=0.591$). AF2-MaxPLDDT had lower $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ in the Non-Redundant split ($p=0.0923$). The large $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ values are consistent with incomplete conformational coverage, as expected for a flexible protein represented by a single conformation.

To contextualise these individual structure results within our broader ensemble experiments, we compared them across all HOIP structural hypotheses (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-bv}F--G). The replicate dispersion and split agreement of every hypothesis are shown in \cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}. Examining the ensembles, AF2-Filtered (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}B) showed the highest local confidence among the five ensembles ($\sigma=6.92$) but the least global confidence ($\sigma=18.52$). AF2-MSAss (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}A) was reported as the most confident ensemble in the Non-Redundant split ($\sigma=11.98$), demonstrating that model parameter optimisation is insensitive to individual structure validity since fitting occurs at the ensemble-average level without distinguishing contributions from individual frames.

To extend our analysis beyond uncertainty within individual splits, we developed a measure of agreement between data splits by computing the RMSD of representative average values of $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ and $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ (\cref{eq:valdx-split-agreement}). Agreement between splits suggests both Non-Redundant and Spatial splits provide similar information on fitting success, which can probe structural deficiencies present at both global and local structural-average levels. With 10 replicates, agreement was computed from median values, which are less sensitive to individual extreme replicates. AF2-MaxPLDDT (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}G) demonstrated the best data split agreement (3.86~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$) overall, with the highest Spatial confidence ($\sigma=5.25$) but second lowest Non-Redundant confidence ($\sigma=38.63$). This pattern suggests a well-folded structure that partially represents the conformational landscape but cannot capture the full extent of global dynamics.

Despite containing only a single structure, AF2-MaxRank (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}F) showed better agreement (7.92~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$) than most ensembles, though extremely high $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ in some Non-Redundant replicates implied large magnitude adjustments in HDX-uptake predictions are required to fit the global conformation.

In terms of median performance, AF2-MaxRank appeared comparable to the AF2-MSAss ensemble (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}A). AF2-MSAss showed smaller, more physically plausible changes across many metrics. Because model parameter optimisation operates solely at the ensemble-average level, multiple explanations exist for this behaviour, including protection factor blurring from averaging over diverse structures or magnitude differences from implausible structural geometries. We therefore conclude that model parameter optimisation cannot provide information at the individual-frame level of an ensemble.

MD-1Start (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}C) showed lower dispersion in the Non-Redundant split than in the Spatial split, though with mediocre agreement (15.86~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$) and poor confidence ($\sigma=16.18$--$17.43$). The increased $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ for MD-10Start (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}D) relative to MD-1Start is consistent with AF2-MaxPLDDT, used as the single starting structure for MD-1Start, representing the more dominant conformation. MD-10Start showed similar confidence ($\sigma=17.27$--$17.44$) to MD-1Start but lower $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$, suggesting both ensembles are similarly plausible despite MD-10Start's additional diversity. However, poor agreement for MD-10Start (18.08~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$) indicates that conformational density estimates remain inadequate.

{\emergencystretch=2em The most promising structural hypothesis without reweighting was MD-TFES (\cref{fig:valdx-hoip-dispersion}E). Despite showing the largest $\text{Work}_\text{density}$ across all hypotheses, MD-TFES demonstrated comparatively reproducible optimisation in both splits, with $\sigma_{\mathrm{NR}}=15.92$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{Sp}}=17.28$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$. In particular, it had the lowest Non-Redundant-split dispersion among the valid ensembles, whose $\sigma_{\mathrm{NR}}$ values ranged from 15.92~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ for MD-TFES to 18.52~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ for AF2-Filtered. MD-TFES showed the best split type agreement amongst ensembles (6.18~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$), contrasting with MD-10Start which exhibited similar $\text{Work}_\text{scale}$ but poor agreement and worse confidence. This suggests that the enhanced sampling strategy of MD-TFES provides improved conformational coverage rather than merely increased structural diversity. Since MD-TFES contains by design the most diverse range of valid conformations, its strong performance across ensembles aligns with the known flexibility and dynamics of HOIP in solution.\par}

It is encouraging that unconstrained model parameter optimisation can be sensitive to both conformational shape and density representativeness. However, valid and invalid ensembles were less well separated by Work Done with model parameter optimisation alone than with regularised structural reweighting.

From these results, we conclude that model parameter optimisation should be performed only on ensembles known to contain plausible structures. When implausible structures are excluded, our results show that using data splits and replicates allows structural ensemble hypotheses to be quantitatively separated and ranked, though with less reliability than MaxEnt reweighting approaches. We therefore suggest that researchers using model parameter optimisation carefully validate ensemble quality before fitting, employ multiple replicates to quantify uncertainty, and preferentially use regularised structural reweighting approaches when computational resources permit.

\section{Population fitting and forward-model calibration}
\label{sec:valdx-calibration}

\subsection{BPTI: comparison of fitting protocols}
\label{sec:valdx-protocol-comparison}

The procedure used to optimise model parameters can influence fitting stability and susceptibility to overfitting. This question carries practical importance because accurate BV-model parameters are needed to use HDX-MS across a range of solution conditions.

BV-parameter optimisation adjusts how residue-specific contact and hydrogen-bond counts contribute to predicted protection factors. Because these features vary among residues, parameter changes alter both the scale and residue-wise pattern of predicted uptake. Experimental pH and labelling temperature enter through intrinsic exchange rates, while deuterium recovery and back-exchange are handled during normalisation. Fitted BV parameters can therefore absorb residual forward-model mismatch. Because their optimisation operates at the ensemble-average level without examining individual structural frames, we hypothesised that this lack of frame-level regularisation would make fitting outcomes more sensitive to the choice of optimisation protocol.

Previous work has suggested that different protocols for optimising ensemble-model parameters produce comparable performance.\supercite{jiaIntegratingHydrogenDeuterium2023} To test this claim, we designed an experiment comparing how different protocols perform when optimising model parameters and frame weights in stages. All protocols were applied to the same BPTI AF2-Filtered ensemble, providing a common starting point for comparing optimisation strategies. AF2-Filtered was selected because it samples broad conformational diversity, including hairpin-opening conformations absent from MD-1Start (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-reweighting}B). However, the preceding analysis showed that its fits were more reproducible under Spatial than under Non-Redundant partitions (\cref{fig:valdx-bpti-dispersion}B). This benchmark therefore assesses protocol performance for a structurally diverse ensemble with remaining population and/or forward-model mismatch; it does not assume complete conformational coverage or globally representative populations. Holding the starting ensemble fixed controls the comparison but does not exclude interactions between protocol choice and ensemble deficiencies.

We tested five distinct optimisation protocols, summarised in \cref{tab:valdx-protocols}. Work accounting for the two-step protocols, including the total distances designated BVafterRW* and RWafterBV*, is defined in \cref{sec:valdx-work}. Full experimental details are provided in \cref{app:valdx-case-protocols}.

These findings show that forward-model parameters meaningfully affect ensemble generalisability. The model parameters, in turn, influence the resulting optimised frame weights. For the BPTI AF2-Filtered ensemble and dataset tested here, BVafterRW (performing MaxEnt reweighting before model parameter optimisation) appears to provide the most stable optimisation strategy. Reweighting first may help address population imbalance in the starting ensemble, so this advantage could depend on its initial population distribution and remaining forward-model mismatch. The comparison therefore supports assessing protocol choice for each ensemble and dataset; establishing whether the same ordering is preferable across ensembles with different coverage and population balance requires additional comparisons.

\subsection{TeaA: response to kinetic misspecification}
\label{sec:valdx-kinetic-misspecification}

Errors in assumed exchange kinetics alter uptake magnitudes as well as curve shapes, which can drive compensatory population changes during reweighting. We imposed a known kinetic mismatch and examined how different fitting protocols responded. Here, fitting BV parameters first restored uptake agreement and limited additional population distortion.

We used the TeaA ISO-Bimodal ensemble to generate synthetic uptake with known target populations of 40\% open and 60\% closed. Across conditions, the structural ensemble and target populations were unchanged, while the ratio of closing to intrinsic exchange rates, $\rho$, was varied across 1000, 1 and 0.01. All datasets were fitted using the standard EX2/BV forward model. This design allowed us to examine the additional fitting requirements introduced by altered exchange kinetics relative to the EX2-like reference condition, $\rho=1000$. Complete generation and fitting procedures are described in \cref{sec:valdx-synthetic,app:valdx-case-kinetic}.

However, the Work Done metrics revealed different requirements at each fitting stage. When BV parameters were fitted first (RWafterBV), subsequent reweighting incurred similar Work Done between $\rho$ values across all Work metrics (\cref{fig:valdx-kinetic-work}D--F). In contrast, when reweighting preceded BV-parameter fitting (BVafterRW), the final parameter optimisation required increasingly large protection-factor changes, similar to the RW-only protocol. $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ increased from $\sim 10.5$ to 52.4~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, while $\text{Work}_{\text{scale}}$ increased from $\sim 0.58$ to 19.0~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ (\cref{fig:valdx-kinetic-work}G--H). The known target populations allowed us to assess the ensemble-level consequences of this staging (\cref{fig:valdx-kinetic-overview}A--B). Fitting BV parameters before reweighting (RWafterBV) limited compensatory population changes. Open State Recovery (\cref{eq:valdx-open-state-recovery}) decreased from 25.7\% to 10.5\% when reweighting was performed with fixed BV parameters (RW-only). Subsequent parameter fitting (BVafterRW) restored uptake agreement by matching the HDX-uptake scale.

RWafterBV showed consistent Work Done across kinetic conditions, which translated to consistent Open State Recovery, with means of 29.2\%, 32.4\% and 27.0\% across the three kinetic conditions. Variation across peptide splits was substantial, with standard deviations of approximately 5.4--12.7 percentage points. At the selected $\gamma$ values, mean RWafterBV open-state populations were 10.8--13.0\%, remaining below the 40\% target. While RWafterBV recovery was low, fitting BV parameters first limited kinetic distortion. Together with the clustering and structural-artefact experiments (\cref{sec:valdx-clustering-results,sec:valdx-brd4-perturbations}), these results build on the principle that better-supported ensemble-models respond more predictably to perturbation.

\subsection{Fitting order and the source of mismatch}
\label{sec:valdx-fitting-order}

The preferred fitting order depends on the source of mismatch. For the BPTI AF2-Filtered ensemble, reweighting before BV-parameter fitting produced the most stable fits, consistent with correcting population imbalance before adjusting the forward model. In the TeaA kinetic benchmark, fitting BV parameters first limited additional population distortion as kinetic mismatch increased, although the target populations remained poorly recovered. Reversing this order restored uptake agreement but retained the population changes introduced during reweighting. When kinetic mismatch is expected, fitting BV parameters first therefore reduces the risk of compensatory population changes. Where the source of mismatch is uncertain, protocol choice requires comparing uptake agreement, Work Done and replicate reproducibility for the candidate ensemble and dataset.

\section{Clustering and diagnostic perturbations}
\label{sec:valdx-perturbations}

\subsection{Sensitivity to ensemble clustering}
\label{sec:valdx-clustering-results}

Having established that BV-model parameter optimisation should be performed on ensembles known to contain plausible structures (\cref{sec:valdx-protocol-comparison}), we set out to determine whether workflows can be made more interpretable by using a small number of basis-set structures to iteratively build up a picture of dynamics. One of the central difficulties in ensemble optimisation is the heavy redundancy of biomolecular simulation trajectories. These large ensembles are challenging to interpret and increase the computational cost of optimisation. While trajectory slicing is commonly used to reduce ensemble size, this may not be ideal for ensembles with non-chronological orderings and can remove important but rare conformations. Clustering reduces ensemble size while retaining structural diversity.

We hypothesised that clustering can be used to assess within-ensemble differences. To test this, we generated proportionally sized ensembles (0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0) and quantified the effect of downsampling on optimisation performance for each BPTI candidate ensemble. Since clustering changes conformational representation and can remove low-population, structurally dissimilar conformations, comparing clustered ensembles against each other allowed us to understand parent ensembles in the context of conformational density coverage. Clustering and fitting details are given in \cref{sec:valdx-decoys,app:valdx-case-clustering}.

To understand the relationship between cluster proportion and ensemble representativeness, we compared $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ across MD-1Start, AF2-Filtered and AF2-MSAss ensembles (\cref{fig:valdx-clustering-sweep}A--C, respectively). Across both data splits, we observed that valid ensembles (MD-1Start and AF2-Filtered) become slightly poorer fits when clustering down to a proportion of 0.01, as expected from loss of conformational density information. In contrast, the implausible AF2-MSAss ensemble showed large improvements in validation error when clustered down from 1.0 to 0.5, and again from 0.05 to 0.001. Within ensembles, AF2-Filtered exhibited closer agreement in trends across splits than MD-1Start, where split types diverged. AF2-Filtered appeared to overfit slightly across the range 0.01 to 0.2 in the Non-Redundant split, while MD-1Start showed overfitting below a proportion of 0.1 in the Spatial split only. Comparing error change between AF2-Filtered and AF2-MSAss suggested that the improvement from removing low-population, structurally dissimilar conformations through clustering can be greater than the loss in density estimates from reduced ensemble weighting.

{\emergencystretch=1em To investigate this observation further, we examined $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ across the cluster sweep (\cref{fig:valdx-clustering-sweep}D--E for Non-Redundant and Spatial splits, respectively). The Non-Redundant split (\cref{fig:valdx-clustering-sweep}D) confirmed that clustering strongly improved AF2-MSAss. At a proportion of 0.001, AF2-MSAss showed similar performance to the unclustered (1.0) AF2-Filtered and MD-1Start ensembles. Across most cluster proportions, MD-TFES exhibited high but consistent $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$, suggesting that this structural hypothesis contains some systematic deficiency. The lower variance for MD-TFES relative to AF2-MSAss distinguished ``oversampled'' from physically invalid ensembles at the global level.\par}

In the Spatial split (\cref{fig:valdx-clustering-sweep}E), MD-TFES proved most sensitive to cluster proportion, while AF2-Filtered, MD-1Start and MD-10Start performed as expected for representative ensembles, requiring additional work at smaller cluster proportions. From these data, MD-10Start showed the most consistent agreement between split types, indicating a stable proposal of conformational density for this protein-ensemble combination.

Because clustering can change an ensemble's structural-density distribution by more than a reduction in weighting, $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ alone is not sufficient to rank ensembles, particularly for MD-TFES, which varied across splits and cluster proportions. We therefore compared the scale changes ($\text{Work}_\text{scale}$) across split types (\cref{fig:valdx-clustering-sweep}F--G). These revealed consistent and strong agreement between data splits for the MD-TFES and MD-10Start ensembles, indicating that there is some systematic deviation in these ensembles related to the overall scale of the dynamics. This deviation is not affected by clustering and is present at both global and local structure levels. This suggested that the sensitivity in $\text{Work}_\text{opt}$ for MD-TFES could be attributed to variability in conformational density when clustering to different proportions.

For AF2-Filtered and MD-1Start ensembles, we observed small differences in uptake scale changes ($\text{Work}_\text{scale}$) over cluster proportions with some split type disagreement, though this was lower than for the AF2-MSAss ensemble. The large variance in scale change and strong positive correlation to cluster proportion for AF2-MSAss in the Non-Redundant split indicated uncertainty and a poor structural hypothesis, respectively. These results provided evidence that within-ensemble differences can be used to build a picture of ensemble quality. However, conclusively distinguishing implausible (AF2-MSAss) from noisy (MD-TFES) structural hypotheses requires multiple data splits and metrics.

From these experiments, we conclude that representative ensembles can be downsampled to proportions between 0.1 and 0.2 with minimal change in the assessed quality. These proportions result in clustered ensembles of approximately 1000 to 2500 structures. Valid ensembles show slight degradation with lower cluster numbers down to a proportion of 0.01, while implausible ensembles (AF2-MSAss) improve, consistent with the removal of low-population, structurally dissimilar conformations. In both cases, this provides grounding to optimise on heavily reduced ensemble sizes for interpretability. We therefore suggest that, for implausible ensembles, care is needed when using small numbers of clusters, as the clustered ensemble may not reflect the full diversity in the parent ensemble. For valid ensembles, a proportion of 0.01 (100 to 127 structures) could result in useful models. In some cases, parent ensembles can be clustered down further to a proportion of 0.001, resulting in a human-interpretable number of 10 to 13 structures for ensemble integration workflows.

One concern with aggressive downsampling is that clustering averages out protection-factor variation across similar structures, potentially making the ensemble less sensitive to errors in the BV forward model. In practice, this trade-off was milder than anticipated. Valid ensembles at proportions of 0.1--0.2 retained discrimination equivalent to the full trajectory, and the loss in forward-model sensitivity was smaller than the gain from changing conformational representation in AF2-MSAss. Whether this favourable balance holds for systems with higher intrinsic flexibility or when a more precise forward model is used remains an open question.

\subsection{BRD4 structural perturbations}
\label{sec:valdx-brd4-perturbations}

Having shown that clustering can probe within-ensemble structural details, we set out to test whether controlled structural artefacts could help distinguish and characterise different aspects of ensemble quality. While clustering simultaneously affects both ensemble composition and structural distribution, we hypothesised that synthetic perturbations with specific, well-defined characteristics would help distinguish contributions from conformational sampling and structural plausibility. By introducing coordinate perturbations and physically invalid geometries, and comparing them with an unmodified control, we could assess how the framework responds to different disruptions of the structural representation.

We chose BRD4 as our test system because it represents a challenging validation scenario. BRD4 consists of two bromodomains separated by loops rich in proline and glycine that confer substantial intrinsic disorder. This architecture produces a broad conformational distribution in solution, making complete sampling unlikely in the ensemble generation experiments performed in this study. The proline-rich regions also limit HDX-MS peptide coverage to the bromodomains themselves. This combination of extensive conformational heterogeneity and sparse experimental coverage represents a realistic test case for probing ensemble quality when validation information is limited.

We designed two types of synthetic structural artefacts as complementary probes of ensemble quality and compared them with an unmodified control that adds copies of existing structures (\cref{tab:valdx-poisoning}; \cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}A, D and G). Each artefact was added at doses of 0.1--50\% to 1000 clustered structures of each unmodified ensemble. Responses were expressed as Z-scores relative to the unmodified ensemble (\cref{eq:valdx-z-score}) and summarised across doses by their standard deviation ($SD$) and Pearson correlation ($R$), with the 50\% dose treated as a negative control (\cref{tab:valdx-brd4-artefact-stats}).

Addition of Gaussian coordinate noise reduced $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}B) for nearly all ensembles tested. This indicates that these perturbed structures require greater statistical work during optimisation to achieve comparable fit quality. Physically valid ensembles exhibited strong linear dose responses ($R=-1.00$) with small response spreads across doses ($SD=0.13$--$0.16$), while AF2-MSAss showed a weaker linear dose response ($R=-0.86$) and greater response spread ($SD=0.61$). This pattern was reinforced by $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ measurements, which increased with artefact proportion for all ensembles (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}C), with particularly pronounced separation between AF2-MSAss and the physically valid ensembles. At the ensemble-average level, however, Gaussian noise artefacts produced only marginal metric differences and therefore provided limited power to discriminate among ensembles.

The more severe coordinate mixing artefacts created stronger effects, producing increased separation between ensembles for both $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ and $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}E--F). AF2-MSAss showed apparent improvement in $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ when these unphysical structures were added, peaking at the 1.0\% level and remaining negative across all proportions below 50\%. In contrast, MD-1Start showed the largest absolute $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ change at 1.0\%, while MD-10Start was most affected at the 0.1\% level, though the difference was small (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}E). AF2-Filtered and MD-TFES showed similar responses to coordinate mixing and were separable from the other ensembles below the 50\% artefact level. While these results showed sensitivity to individual structures, coordinate mixing provided primarily binary discrimination between plausible and implausible ensembles rather than fine-grained quality assessment.

The unmodified control produced diverse response patterns among ensembles, although it added no new conformations (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}H--I). At the 50\% dose, its responses stayed within the spread of its lower doses, whereas Gaussian noise and coordinate mixing moved every ensemble far beyond theirs. These responses support attributing the large 50\% artefact shifts to structural corruption rather than to the addition of unchanged structures. Unlike the two artefacts, the control did not distinguish physically valid ensembles from the implausible AF2-MSAss ensemble, as expected for an operation that adds no new conformations.

Because the control adds no new conformations, its responses show how sensitive each fit is to adding copies of existing structures, which raises their prior weight. Analysis of $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}I) initially suggested MD-10Start improved with addition of copied structures; however, joint consideration with $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ (\cref{fig:valdx-brd4-artefacts}H) revealed overfitting behaviour.

{\emergencystretch=1em AF2-Filtered was unique in showing improvement by $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$, but exhibited minimal changes in $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ below a 10\% dose. AF2-Filtered had the largest $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ response spread ($SD=0.70$), whereas MD-10Start had the largest $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ response spread ($SD=0.62$). MD-1Start had small response spreads ($SD=0.06$ and $0.12$, respectively) and positive dose dependence ($R=0.98$ and $0.70$, respectively). MD-TFES showed weak positive dose dependence in $\Delta \text{MSE}_{\text{opt}}$ ($R=0.23$) and moderate negative dose dependence in $\text{Work}_{\text{opt}}$ ($R=-0.58$), reinforcing the need to interpret both metrics jointly.\par}

MD-1Start had smaller response spreads than MD-10Start under the unmodified control. Because the protocols differ in both trajectory length and starting-state diversity, this experiment cannot attribute that difference to either factor.

From these experiments, we conclude that structural-artefact responses provide complementary information about conformational sampling and structural plausibility, although these contributions are not fully separable. Within physically valid ensembles, this experiment showed that ensemble reweighting can detect and quantify the effects of individual perturbed structures at the global optimisation level. Despite controlled artefacts and normalised metrics, making definitive statements about relative ensemble quality proved challenging, particularly when considering ensembles in isolation. Even for a single artefact type, the response patterns were complex and required multiple complementary metrics to confidently evaluate structural hypotheses. We propose that the controlled introduction of synthetic structural artefacts can reveal specific deficiencies in ensemble quality. However, this study also showed limits to the sensitivity of the BV model in differentiating between ensembles and simple coordinate perturbations. Strong claims about ensemble accuracy therefore need orthogonal validation.
