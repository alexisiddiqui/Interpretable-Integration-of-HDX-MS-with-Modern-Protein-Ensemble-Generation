\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:jaxka-motivation}


Protonation links a protein's chemical environment to its response to pH. Burial, hydrogen bonding and nearby charges alter the relative stability of a titratable group's protonated and deprotonated forms, shifting its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ from the value measured in an unstructured peptide.\supercite{grimsleySummaryMeasuredPK2009} Binding can change these interactions, so the same group may titrate differently in the free protein and the complex. Differences between these states connect protonation to the pH dependence of binding affinity.\supercite{onufrievProtonationPKChanges2013}

A backbone-based $\mathrm{p}K_a$ predictor would be compatible with sequence-design workflows built around ProteinMPNN and its variants, including SolubleMPNN, PottsMPNN, and the acidophile and alkaliphile fine-tunes examined here.\supercite{dauparasRobustDeepLearningbased2022,birnbaumBeyondNativeSequence2026,dillonDecodingPhysicochemicalBasis2026} Using the same backbone coordinates would allow protonation objectives to score and rank mutations proposed during inverse folding, without reconstructing side chains for every candidate. The prediction must also accommodate mutations that introduce or remove titratable groups, so that candidate substitutions can be assessed across the sequence. We therefore investigate whether backbone geometry and sequence retain sufficient information to predict $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values and their changes on binding, and how these predictions can inform sequence selection during protein design.

\subsection{Computational Titration Models}
\label{sec:jaxka-titration-models}

Assessing what a backbone predictor must retain first requires considering how existing methods describe the local chemical environment. PROPKA estimates a group's $\mathrm{p}K_a$ by adding empirical contributions from desolvation, hydrogen bonding and charge--charge interactions to a model-compound value. These contributions make individual predictions interpretable and inexpensive to calculate from an atomic structure.\supercite{liVeryFastEmpirical2005} Their interpretation nevertheless depends on the conformation supplied, particularly when protonation accompanies substantial structural rearrangement.\supercite{olssonPROPKA3ConsistentTreatment2011}

For a given conformation, protonation at one site changes the electrostatic environment experienced by the others. PypKa accounts for these interactions by combining Poisson--Boltzmann calculations of electrostatic energies with Monte Carlo sampling of protonation states. Sampling across pH yields titration curves from which $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values are obtained. The calculation retains a fixed protein conformation, while its dielectric model approximates the response of the protein and solvent. Sampling protonation states therefore captures their coupling within the supplied structure, but does not explicitly describe structural rearrangements accompanying protonation.\supercite{reisPypKaFlexiblePython2020}

When protonation and conformation influence one another, both must be sampled to describe their coupled response to pH. Constant-pH molecular dynamics allows protonation states to change as the protein moves, whereas conventional molecular dynamics holds the assigned states fixed. This permits structural changes to alter protonation preferences and changes in protonation to alter the interactions governing motion. Its predictions nevertheless depend on the physical model and sufficient sampling of both processes.\supercite{deoliveiraConstantPHMolecular2022} Repeating this sampling for candidate mutations adds computational cost, motivating faster predictors for sequence screening.

Learned predictors reduce the cost of evaluating a supplied structure by mapping its chemical environment directly to a $\mathrm{p}K_a$ estimate. pKAI uses a neural network to predict $\mathrm{p}K_a$ shifts from the residue type and a distance-weighted representation of nearby nitrogen, oxygen and sulfur atoms. Training on PypKa calculations allows it to approximate those predictions without repeating the electrostatic calculation and protonation-state sampling. Its training target is therefore a computational prediction. The related pKAI+ model uses the same theoretical labels but penalises large predicted shifts, favouring values closer to the model-compound reference.\supercite{reisFastInterpretableDeep2022} Agreement with these labels and agreement with experimental $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values must consequently be assessed separately.

Experimental measurements can also supervise learned $\mathrm{p}K_a$ predictors directly. The KaML models use CatBoost decision trees and graph attention networks trained against the PKAD-3 experimental dataset, with theoretical pretraining and structural augmentation used to extend the available training information. On the authors' held-out tests, both models improve on PROPKA3, with the tree model giving the more accurate predictions. Comparisons with reported pKAI and PypKa errors use different test sets, however, so they do not establish a ranking under common evaluation conditions.\supercite{shenKaMLsPredictingProtein2025} Assessing these approaches together requires the same held-out measurements and explicit separation from their training data.

Even on a common test set, overall error can conceal whether a predictor captures the effect of the protein environment. Experimental datasets contain many solvent-exposed groups whose $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values lie close to their model-compound references. A null model that assigns these reference values without considering structure can therefore achieve errors comparable to more elaborate predictors.\supercite{stantonBenchmarkingPKaPrediction2008,reisFastInterpretableDeep2022} Improvement over this baseline tests whether structural information contributes beyond residue identity, while separate assessment of large shifts tests performance where the environment substantially changes protonation preferences. We therefore compare predictors against the model-compound baseline and distinguish overall accuracy from accuracy on strongly shifted sites.

Scoring mutations for pH-dependent binding requires predicting how association changes protonation preferences. We therefore train jax-Ka against both individual-state $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values and the differences between bound and free partners. Each complex is compared with its isolated partners held at their bound conformations, so the predicted change isolates the effect of the partner without including structural relaxation. Paired supervision makes this binding-induced shift an explicit target for a predictor that reads backbone geometry and sequence. The resulting scores allow candidate substitutions to be assessed in the context of the interaction being designed.
