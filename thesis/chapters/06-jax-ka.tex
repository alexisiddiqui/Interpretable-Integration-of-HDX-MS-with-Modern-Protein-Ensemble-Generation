\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:jaxka-motivation}

Protonation links a protein's chemical environment to its response to pH. Burial, hydrogen bonding and nearby charges alter the relative stability of a titratable group's protonated and deprotonated forms, and shift its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ from the value measured in an unstructured peptide.\supercite{grimsleySummaryMeasuredPK2009} Binding can change these interactions, so the same group may titrate differently in the free protein and the complex. Differences between these states connect protonation to the pH dependence of binding affinity.\supercite{onufrievProtonationPKChanges2013} pH dependence is therefore a design target for applications ranging from extremophile adaptation to pH-responsive therapeutics.

Designing this pH dependence requires reconciling the information used for sequence generation with that needed to predict protonation. Protonation depends on side-chain environments, while inverse-folding models such as ProteinMPNN propose sequences from backbone coordinates without specifying side-chain conformations.\supercite{dauparasRobustDeepLearningbased2022} While full-atom models do exist, variants such as SolubleMPNN and PottsMPNN have been proven effective in producing expressable and functional proteins.\supercite{dauparasRobustDeepLearningbased2022,birnbaumBeyondNativeSequence2026} A $\mathrm{p}K_a$ predictor that accepts backbone coordinates and sequence could help rank their proposed mutations without relying on minimised side-chain structures.

\subsection{Computational Titration Models}
\label{sec:jaxka-titration-models}

PROPKA models how chemical information that such a backbone predictor would need to infer. It estimates a group's $\mathrm{p}K_a$ by adding empirical contributions from desolvation, hydrogen bonding and charge--charge interactions to a model-compound value. These contributions make individual predictions interpretable and inexpensive to calculate from an atomic structure.\supercite{liVeryFastEmpirical2005} The predictions depend on the supplied conformation and can fail when protonation accompanies structural rearrangement.\supercite{olssonPROPKA3ConsistentTreatment2011}

Within a fixed conformation, protonation at one site also changes the electrostatic environment experienced by the others. To account for interaction coupling, PypKa combines Poisson--Boltzmann calculations of electrostatic energies with Monte Carlo sampling of protonation states. Sampling across pH yields titration curves from which $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values are obtained. The calculation holds the protein conformation fixed, while its dielectric model approximates the response of the protein and solvent. It captures coupling between protonation states within the supplied structure, but does not explicitly sample structural rearrangements.\supercite{reisPypKaFlexiblePython2020}

When those rearrangements alter protonation preferences, conformation and protonation must be sampled together. Constant-pH molecular dynamics allows protonation states to change as the protein moves, whereas conventional molecular dynamics holds the assigned states fixed. Changes in protonation can then alter the interactions governing motion, and motion can change the relative stability of protonation states. Accuracy depends on the physical model and sufficient sampling of both processes.\supercite{deoliveiraConstantPHMolecular2022} For mutation screening, our aim is to score candidate sequences without running these coupled simulations for every proposal.

pKAI accelerates fixed-structure prediction by learning the relationship between atomic environments and calculated $\mathrm{p}K_a$ shifts. The fully-connected neural network uses the residue type and a distance-weighted representation of nearby nitrogen, oxygen and sulfur atoms. Training on PypKa calculations allows it to approximate those predictions without repeating the electrostatic calculation and protonation-state sampling. The related pKAI+ model uses the same theoretical labels but penalises large predicted shifts. This penalty biases predictions towards the model-compound reference. Agreement with the computational labels and agreement with experimental $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values remain separate tests.\supercite{reisFastInterpretableDeep2022}

Experimental measurements can also supervise learned predictors directly. KaML uses CatBoost decision trees and graph attention networks trained against PKAD-3. For the graph model, pretraining uses $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values calculated by constant-pH molecular dynamics, while additional AlphaFold structures expand the structural examples associated with residues whose experimental shifts exceed two $\mathrm{p}K_a$ units in magnitude. On the authors' held-out tests, both models improve on PROPKA3, and the tree model is more accurate. Comparisons with published pKAI and PypKa errors use different test sets.\supercite{shenKaMLsPredictingProtein2025} A direct comparison requires a common experimental test set after removing entries used to train any of the models.

Even on a common test set, overall error can conceal whether a predictor captures the effect of the protein environment. Experimental datasets contain many solvent-exposed groups whose $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values lie close to their model-compound references. A null model that assigns these reference values without considering structure can achieve errors comparable to more elaborate predictors.\supercite{stantonBenchmarkingPKaPrediction2008,reisFastInterpretableDeep2022} We compare predictors against this baseline and assess large shifts separately to distinguish overall accuracy from the ability to recover departures from model-compound behaviour.

For binding, the relevant departure is the change between the complex and its isolated partners. We investigate whether backbone geometry and sequence retain enough information to predict both individual-state $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values and these binding-induced shifts. Training pairs each complex with its isolated partners held at their bound conformations, so the comparison isolates the predicted effect of the partner without including structural relaxation. Paired supervision makes this change an explicit prediction target.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:jaxka-methods}

\subsection{Prediction Targets and Datasets}
\label{sec:jaxka-states}

For each titratable site $i$, we express its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ relative to the model-compound value for its chemical group, $g(i)$. In structural state $s$, the environmental shift is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{s}
    = \mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{s}
    - \mathrm{p}K_{a,\mathrm{model},g(i)}.
    \label{eq:jaxka-model-compound-shift}
\end{equation}
This reference separates the group's baseline protonation preference from the effect of its protein environment.\supercite{liVeryFastEmpirical2005,olssonPROPKA3ConsistentTreatment2011} The predictor estimates the shift, and adding the fixed reference value recovers the predicted $\mathrm{p}K_a$. Reference values and conversion conventions are given in \cref{app:jaxka-references}.

For a complex comprising partners $A$ and $B$, we evaluate the assembled complex $AB$ and each isolated partner without changing its coordinates. Let $F_i$ denote the isolated partner containing site $i$. The binding-induced shift is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}\mathrm{p}K_{a,i}
    = \mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{AB} - \mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{F_i}
    = \Delta\mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{AB} - \Delta\mathrm{p}K_{a,i}^{F_i}.
    \label{eq:jaxka-binding-shift}
\end{equation}
The reference cancels because the same chemical group is compared in both states. A positive value means that association raises the site's $\mathrm{p}K_a$. Here, ``free'' denotes the isolated partner at its bound conformation, so the comparison measures the predicted effect of adding the partner without including conformational relaxation.

Training combined individual-state labels from pKPDB with paired-state labels generated for PINDER protein complexes. The deposited pKPDB values were calculated with PypKa and supplied absolute-$\mathrm{p}K_a$ targets.\supercite{reisPKPDBProteinData2022} PINDER supplied complexes from which we constructed matched bound and isolated-partner states for labelling with released pKAI.\supercite{kovtunPINDERProteinInteraction2024} The two datasets thus supplied different computational targets. Agreement with PINDER labels measures reproduction of pKAI predictions, while agreement with pKPDB measures reproduction of its historical PypKa calculations. 

Preparation of our datasets preserved the matched-state comparison while excluding targets whose environments could not be represented reliably. Bound and separated states came from the same repaired complex. We excluded sites affected by reconstructed residues, nearby removed nonprotein components or missing protein segments, using masks calibrated through component and residue deletion tests. The exclusions applied to individual targets rather than discarding every incomplete structure. Their distances define operational limits under the calibration predictors, rather than guarantees that omitted material has no effect beyond a cutoff. Deletion-test results are shown in \cref{fig:jaxka-component-cutoffs,fig:jaxka-gap-cutoffs}; preparation procedures, tolerances and distance rules are specified in \cref{app:jaxka-data-preparation}.

To limit sequence overlap with independent evaluation, training candidates were screened against the frozen benchmark and experimental reference proteins, and reserved PDB entries were excluded. Whole-chain and fragment comparisons covered related sequences. Antibody--antigen complexes used a separate screen on antibody complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), so framework similarity alone did not exclude antibodies with different binding loops. Antigen chains retained whole-chain screening, as did antibody heavy--light interfaces, where the framework participates in binding. The identity thresholds and annotation-transfer procedure are given in \cref{app:jaxka-sequence-separation}.

Development validation remained separate from these independent evaluations. For production models, we reserved 800 pKPDB groups and 800 PINDER clusters for checkpoint selection, with deliberately shared sequence coverage between the two validation sets. Training excluded their groups and related chains. Fixed validation membership was retained across nested training fractions, so changing the amount of training data did not also change the selection set. Fractions referred to eligible groups, with all constituent structures retained together. This controlled the population used in data-scale comparisons; it did not make development validation an independent test of choices made during model development (\cref{app:jaxka-validation}). The earlier pKAI controls used a separate frozen validation cohort, specified in \cref{app:jaxka-pkai-inputs}.

\subsection{Backbone Model and Prediction Heads}
\label{sec:jaxka-ogqt}

The retained orientation Graph Query Transformer (oGQT) predicts from backbone geometry and sequence using a residue graph and a query at every residue. Residue nodes carry amino-acid identity and backbone dihedral features. Attention between spatial neighbours combines these features with their relative geometry, allowing each residue's representation to depend on its molecular environment. Query tokens read the encoded residues and exchange information with nearby queries before producing site predictions. The bound and separated calculations share model parameters, so their difference is not a comparison between independently trained predictors (\cref{fig:jaxka-model-overview}).

\input{../figures/jaxka-model-overview/figure}

A predicted $\mathrm{p}K_a$ should change with its molecular environment, but not with the position or orientation of the protein as a whole. We built this distinction into the geometric inputs by describing each residue's surroundings in a local frame defined by its backbone atoms. When the protein rotates, these frames rotate with it. The frames are therefore equivariant, while directions and relative orientations expressed within them remain invariant to global rotation and translation. This construction preserves the geometry of the molecular environment without requiring structures to be aligned.

Two residue-attention layers share information over the spatial graph. Learned relation maps distinguish sequence neighbours, other residues in the same chain and residues in another chain, so sequence adjacency and spatial contact need not contribute in the same way. Query updates use a smaller neighbourhood than residue encoding to limit the cost of predicting at every position, while retaining information gathered over the wider graph. Exact relation classes, neighbourhoods and the earlier architecture used in representation comparisons are specified in \cref{app:jaxka-architecture}.

Each query combines the encoded residue with a learned amino-acid identity embedding and supplies separate side-chain, N-terminal and C-terminal outputs. A titratability head predicts whether each output corresponds to an ionisable group. Placing queries at every residue retains a readout when a substitution introduces or removes a titratable site, so sequence scoring is not restricted to the starting set of titratable residues. This separates the availability of a prediction from the presence of a training label. Only eligible labelled sites supervise the $\mathrm{p}K_a$ outputs.

For the separated-state pass, cross-chain edges are masked while coordinates and model parameters remain fixed. Each partner is then encoded independently. A bounded scalar head predicts the environmental shift, to which the group-specific reference is added. Subtracting separated from bound predictions gives the binding shift in \cref{eq:jaxka-binding-shift}. Auxiliary heads predict residue burial and cross-chain contact scores. The contact head combines the two residue representations symmetrically, so exchanging their order leaves the score unchanged. It can score pairs from either the complex representation or the independently encoded partners.

The separated contact readout combines representations formed without partner context. Its predictions therefore test whether each partner's backbone geometry and sequence retain information about the eventual interface. The relative placement of the two proteins in the assembled complex is unavailable to this readout.

Within our experiments, controlled pKAI comparisons tested which information a backbone predictor needed before attributing differences to the graph architecture. Removing side-chain coordinates tested their contribution within the pKAI architecture; adding neighbouring residue identities tested whether sequence information could compensate. For each representation, pKPDB-only training was compared with joint state-and-shift supervision from pKPDB and PINDER. These comparisons used models trained from scratch, separately from the released pKAI and pKAI+ comparators. Their input encodings and training objective are specified in \cref{app:jaxka-pkai-inputs,app:jaxka-pkai-objective}.

\subsection{Training and Model Selection}
\label{sec:jaxka-training}

Errors in the bound and separated states can cancel or reinforce when their difference is taken. We trained against both the individual-state $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values and binding-induced shifts so that agreement with either target alone was insufficient. The primary objective summed mean-squared errors for pKPDB predictions, PINDER state predictions averaged between bound and separated states, and PINDER binding-shift predictions. The binding-shift term propagated gradients through both state predictions. Errors were averaged within structures before averaging across structures, preventing those with more labelled sites from dominating the objective (\cref{app:jaxka-primary-losses}).

Eligibility for $\mathrm{p}K_a$ supervision depended on the prepared labels and masks, independently of predicted titratability. For example, disulfide-bonded cysteines were excluded. Neither PypKa nor pKAI models arginine $\mathrm{p}K_a$ shifts, leaving arginine without targets in the training datasets. It remained a positive example for titratability classification but was excluded from $\mathrm{p}K_a$ supervision. The classification loss was separate from the regression loss, so predicting low titratability could not suppress an eligible site's $\mathrm{p}K_a$ error. No additional weighting by burial, interface proximity or shift magnitude was applied to these regression errors.

Auxiliary supervision connected the shared representation to solvent exposure and interface geometry. Burial prediction used relative solvent accessibility (RSA) in each state, while a paired term supervised its change on binding. Contact prediction used distance-based targets from the bound structure for both bound and separated representations. The separated branch therefore had to predict contacts without observing the assembled interface. These tasks extended supervision beyond titratable sites and distinguished residue exposure from the pairwise relationships that define an interface.

Distillation provided a second source of contact supervision for the separated branch. The current bound contact probabilities supplied soft targets, with gradients stopped through those targets while the bound branch retained its own supervised loss. This self distilation encouraged independently encoded partners to recover contact information available in the assembled complex. The baseline multi-task recipe (run4) combined these contact and distillation terms with the primary $\mathrm{p}K_a$, titratability and burial objectives. Complete losses, masks, gradient paths and coefficients are defined in \cref{app:jaxka-losses}.

The hotspot-masking recipe (hmask) added within-complex ranking and masking guided by PeSTo.\supercite{krappPeSToParameterfreeGeometric2023} Ranking compared candidate contacts for an exposed anchor residue against a normalised distribution of distance-based targets, so the model had to distinguish alternatives within the same complex. Masking retained identities at selected exposed residues with high PeSTo interface probabilities on one chain and removed its other identity features. Backbone geometry and the other chain's sequence remained available. Only separated-partner contact supervision, distillation and ranking used the masked input; $\mathrm{p}K_a$, burial and titratability predictions retained complete sequences. Inference also used complete sequences.

Within-complex ranking compares residues from the same observed partner. It does not directly supervise discrimination between that partner and a different protein. This distinction governs the interpretation of subsequent partner-swapping and external binder/decoy evaluations, which ask whether scores identify the correct partner.

The burial-weighted ranking recipe (dRSA) changed the ranking targets while retaining hmask augmentation. Each distance-based target was weighted by the smaller burial increase of its two residues before normalisation across candidates. Contacts received little weight when either residue underwent little burial on binding. Comparing hmask with dRSA therefore tested whether structural burial changes improved ranking supervision under the same input augmentation. The full recipe definitions are given in \cref{tab:jaxka-recipe-coefficients}.

All three recipes were trained jointly on pKPDB and PINDER. Checkpoints were selected by the sum of PINDER validation state MAE and interface binding-shift MAE. Interface evaluation used the stored 10~\AA{} partner-proximity mask, independently of the burial-based ranking targets. Contact and partner-ranking metrics were evaluated at that checkpoint, so selection remained tied to $\mathrm{p}K_a$ prediction as interface objectives were added. We retained the lowest-error checkpoint rather than the last epoch. Optimiser settings, stopping rules and standard and extended schedules are specified in \cref{app:jaxka-optimisation,app:jaxka-extended-schedule}.

\subsection{Sequence Guidance and Application Design}
\label{sec:jaxka-sequence-sampling}

Surface-charge steering tested whether an explicit protonation score induced the sequence preferences learned by extremophile fine-tuning. GQT or pKAI predictions scored the isolated binder's surface charge, favouring positive charge at pH 3 or negative charge at pH 10 (\cref{eq:jaxka-surface-charge}). During Gibbs sampling, scaled scores for candidate substitutions were added to vanilla ProteinMPNN's conditional log-probabilities before each residue was sampled. Both models remained fixed. AcidSecMPNN and AlkSecMPNN designs were compared with autoregressive vanilla ProteinMPNN, while guided designs were compared with unsteered Gibbs sampling. Each method was assessed against its own sampling control.

We matched each guided arm's change in formal surface charge to that of its corresponding fine-tune, relative to their respective controls. The statistic was $(N_K+N_R-N_D-N_E)/N_{\mathrm{surface}}$ on a fixed surface set. Agreement in this statistic was imposed by design, leaving residue composition, replacement preferences and altered positions to be compared. Arms used the same matched backbone subsets, and summaries gave each design challenge equal weight. Sampling settings, cohort exclusions and statistical definitions are given in \cref{app:jaxka-surface-charge,app:jaxka-design-statistics}.

The initial chemistry comparison used isolated binders for sequence generation and scoring. Its matched subset contained six backbones, reduced to five for the pKAI alkaliphile arm because one comparison could not be matched. For complex evaluation, sequence generation was repeated with fixed target context and charge changes were matched again against the corresponding controls. The surface-charge objective continued to score the isolated binder, so adding target context to ProteinMPNN did not itself introduce a binding objective.

Structural evaluation distinguished recovery of the binder fold from recovery of its binding pose. Complex comparisons were restricted to TNF and TCR/pMHC because only these systems passed source-sequence pose-recovery controls. Designs generated with fixed target context had to recover binder C$\alpha$ RMSD within 5~\AA{} after target alignment and binder ipTM of at least 0.5. Each arm contained eight independent sequence designs with eight refolds per design. Refolds measured consistency of prediction and were not independent sequence designs (\cref{app:jaxka-design-structural-evaluation}).

Binding responses were evaluated only for structurally accepted TNF refolds. After restrained minimisation, PROPKA, pKAI and pKAI+ predictions for the complex and separated partners gave proton-linkage estimates of $\log_{10}[K_D(h)/K_D(7.4)]$. Positive whole-complex values indicate predicted weaker binding at pH $h$. Each design contributed its mean over valid accepted refolds. Binder-site contributions were also reported separately, without interpreting them as complete affinity predictions. These comparisons remain predictions conditional on structural acceptance (\cref{app:jaxka-design-binding-evaluation}).

Proton-linkage steering instead targeted the pH dependence of binding during generation. On TNF, the GQT objective favoured a larger predicted dissociation constant at pH 6.0 than at pH 7.4 by comparing bound and separated protonation (\cref{eq:jaxka-linkage-score}). Binder and target sites contributed; the target sequence stayed fixed, but its bound-state predictions could change with the binder. Steering strength was swept independently of formal-charge matching to assess the trade-off between predicted binding response and pose recovery. This application used one refold per design, followed by the same three-predictor evaluation of accepted structures (\cref{app:jaxka-proton-linkage}).

\subsection{Backbone pKa and Binding-Shift Prediction}
\label{sec:jaxka-backbone-results}

% Source: projects/4-jax-Ka/code/experiments/3_paired_complex/48_pkai_joint_scale.md,

% One training seed per arm. Supporting tables: figures/jaxka-pkai-siamese/table.tex.
To obtain basellines for both our datasets and siamese loss, we retrained pKAI. Restricting pKAI to backbone inputs increased error in both individual-state and binding-shift prediction. With the full training pool and pKPDB-only supervision, pKPDB validation MSE rose from 0.319 with all-atom inputs to 1.045 with backbone inputs; interface binding-shift MSE rose from 0.059 to 0.467 (\cref{tab:jaxka-pkai-joint}). Both representations retained the query's titratable-group identity, but the backbone representation omitted the neighbouring side-chain atoms that distinguish local chemical environments. We tested whether joint state-and-shift supervision could compensate for the missing side-chain information.

Joint supervision benefited the all-atom model more than the backbone model. With native atom-class inputs, adding PINDER state and binding-shift targets lowered all-atom pKPDB validation MSE from 0.319 to 0.287 and interface binding-shift MSE from 0.059 to 0.034. Backbone errors changed little, from 1.045 to 1.043 and from 0.467 to 0.453, respectively (\cref{tab:jaxka-pkai-joint}). Joint training improved both tasks when side-chain coordinates were available but did little to compensate for their absence. PINDER state and binding-shift losses were added together, so this comparison does not isolate paired supervision. We therefore tested a graph representation that explicitly encodes backbone geometry.

% Source: projects/4-jax-Ka/code/experiments/4_backbone/07_handoff.md,
% "Results so far"; three-seed means for bb64, orient, dh and A.
Explicit backbone geometry improved prediction within the earlier site-query graph model, with most of the gain coming from dihedral features. We compared dihedrals and relative residue orientations separately and together across three training seeds. At full training data, the mean sum of PINDER state MAE and interface binding-shift MAE fell from 0.6503 to 0.6238 with dihedrals, compared with 0.6459 with orientation features alone. Combining both gave 0.6212 (\cref{tab:jaxka-geometry}). We retained the combined representation, although its advantage over dihedrals alone was small relative to variation between training runs.

% Source: figures/jaxka-relational-edges/fig.json, three-seed selection means.
Relation-specific transformations improved prediction at 50\% of the training pool. We distinguished sequence neighbours, other same-chain neighbours and cross-chain neighbours using six relation maps in each residue-attention layer. Across three seeds, mean selection error fell from 0.6494 to 0.6324. A wider model with a similar parameter count scored 0.6447, while using one shared map per layer gave 0.6427 (\cref{fig:jaxka-relational-edges}). The reduction occurred in both PINDER state MAE (0.4102 to 0.3992) and interface binding-shift MAE (0.2393 to 0.2332), so the lower selection error did not arise from improving one target at the expense of the other. Distinguishing residue relationships improved prediction beyond increasing width or applying one shared transformation.

Expanding attention beyond the residue graph added computational cost with little change in prediction error. For the 4/1 model, unrestricted second-layer attention changed selection error from 0.6349 to 0.6344 in the seed-17 comparison (\cref{fig:jaxka-relational-edges}). In the PINDER batch-step benchmark at 1,536 residues, measured peak GPU memory increased by 28\% and step time by 29\% (\cref{fig:jaxka-relational-memory}). Relation maps themselves required no additional attended pairs. We retained local attention, since this comparison gave little reason to expand the neighbourhood.

Agreement with computational labels did not translate into a consistent advantage over the model-compound null on experimental $\mathrm{p}K_a$s. On 1,421 PKAD-3 entries with predictions available across the compared methods, the all-residue run4 model had MAE 0.82 and RMSE 1.42, compared with 0.84 and 1.31 for the null (\cref{tab:jaxka-experimental-common}). Its small advantage in MAE was reversed under RMSE, which gives greater weight to large prediction errors. We therefore examined performance by experimental shift magnitude to distinguish agreement near the model-compound reference from recovery of larger environmental shifts.

Large experimental shifts remained poorly predicted. Across the 1,489 entries for which run4 returned predictions, RMSE was 0.63 for experimental shifts below one $\mathrm{p}K_a$ unit in magnitude, 1.13 for shifts between one and two units, and 3.85 for shifts of at least two units (\cref{tab:jaxka-experimental-diagnostics}\,(a)). Agreement near the model-compound reference therefore did not extend to sites with strongly altered protonation preferences.

\subsection{Sequence Preferences at Matched Surface-Charge Changes}
\label{sec:jaxka-sequence-results}

% Source: kb/experiments/027-extremophile-vs-pka-surface.md,
% "Different surface chemistry at matched charge changes".
Matching changes in formal surface charge revealed different residue preferences under extremophile fine-tuning and $\mathrm{p}K_a$-guided steering. We compared each method against its own sampling control, matching the signed charge change so that the comparison could resolve how that change was achieved. Acidophile fine-tuning raised formal charge mainly by removing glutamate, whereas GQT and pKAI steering predominantly added lysine. Both alkaliphile routes enriched acidic residues, but the fine-tune favoured glutamate more strongly and depleted proline, while steering distributed enrichment across aspartate and glutamate (\cref{fig:jaxka-extremophile-chemistry}\,(A--F)).

The acidophile glutamate-to-glutamine preference illustrates this chemical distinction. Fine-tuning favoured this exchange at corresponding surface positions, replacing a terminal carboxylate with an amide while retaining a similar side-chain scaffold (1.91 net replacements per 100 surface positions; \cref{fig:jaxka-extremophile-chemistry}\,(G)). Both steering predictors showed a small preference in the reverse direction and instead favoured alanine-to-lysine exchanges (2.55 for GQT and 2.68 for pKAI). Removing a carboxylate and introducing a basic side chain can produce the same formal-charge change, but they alter hydrogen-bonding chemistry and the response to pH differently.

Proline depletion exposed a larger difference that extended to the backbone. Acidophile fine-tuning favoured proline-to-alanine exchanges, whereas alkaliphile fine-tuning strongly favoured proline-to-glutamate exchanges (3.43 and 6.96 net replacements per 100 surface positions, respectively; \cref{fig:jaxka-extremophile-chemistry}\,(G, H)). The corresponding alkaliphile preferences under GQT and pKAI were only 0.83 and 0.68. Both replacements restore a backbone N--H donor, permitting hydrogen bonds with protein acceptors as well as water. Proline depletion therefore changes the available backbone interactions alongside surface chemistry.

% Interpretation: kb/chapters/06-jax-ka.md, proline and local-strain argument.
The proline substitutions favoured by fine-tuning suggest a possible change in the balance between local strain and stabilising interactions. Removing proline releases its ring constraint, while restoring a backbone donor can support intramolecular hydrogen bonds that constrain the resulting conformation. Such interactions could favour a locally strained conformation when their energetic contribution, together with solvent interactions, compensates for that strain.

Despite these differences in surface chemistry, binder-only refolding largely recovered the design backbone. Across 496 analysed designs, 494 predictions exceeded a self-consistency TM-score of 0.5, with challenge-weighted means of 0.92--0.96 across the matched arms (\cref{fig:jaxka-extremophile-monomer-refolding}; \cref{app:jaxka-design-results}). Recovery of the binder fold leaves unresolved whether these sequence changes preserve the binding pose and produce a predicted pH-dependent binding response.

\subsection{Predicted pH-Dependent Binding and Pose Retention}
\label{sec:jaxka-linkage-results}

% Source: kb/experiments/027-extremophile-vs-pka-surface.md,
% "Complex aware steering produces predicted pH selective binding".
Target context alone did not make surface-charge steering select pH-dependent binding. On TNF, designs generated with the target present and retaining an accepted pose showed approximately zero binder-site contribution towards release at pH 6 under pKAI and pKAI+. Acidophile fine-tuning retained a modest predicted response through interface histidines, with median binder-site contributions to $\log_{10}[K_D(6.0)/K_D(7.4)]$ of $+0.43$, $+0.27$ and $+0.19$ under PROPKA, pKAI and pKAI+, respectively (\cref{fig:jaxka-complex-linkage-selectivity}\,(C--E)). A charge objective acting on the separated binder at pH 3 or 10 does not reward the difference between bound and free protonation that determines this binding response.

To reward that difference directly, we replaced surface-charge steering with a proton-linkage objective comparing bound and separated states across pH 7.4 and 6.0. Increasing steering strength raised the GQT linkage score and histidine content, but reduced pose retention (\cref{fig:jaxka-complex-linkage-selectivity}\,(A, B)). At strength 0.05, the median binder-site score remained near zero and seven of eight designs retained the pose. At strength 0.8, the score reached 7.25, but none retained the pose. The objective could therefore be increased beyond the range compatible with recovery of the intended interface.

% Sources: figures/jaxka-complex-linkage-selectivity/steering_response.csv
% and binding_per_design.csv, 24-design follow-up populations.
Intermediate steering produced designs combining pose retention with a larger predicted pH response. In follow-up populations of 24 designs, 12 retained the pose at strength 0.15, compared with four at 0.20 (\cref{fig:jaxka-complex-linkage-selectivity}\,(B)). Among retained designs at 0.15, median binder-site contributions were $+1.50$, $+0.96$ and $+0.65$ under PROPKA, pKAI and pKAI+, exceeding the rescored source complex under all three predictors (\cref{fig:jaxka-complex-linkage-selectivity}\,(C--E)). Nine of the 24 generated designs both retained the pose and exceeded the source response under every checking predictor. Increasing steering to 0.20 raised the PROPKA median but reduced the pKAI and pKAI+ medians, alongside the lower pose yield. These are predicted binder-site contributions conditional on structural acceptance, rather than measured affinity changes.

The useful design criterion is therefore the combination of predicted linkage, structural compatibility and corroboration by checking predictors. Increasing the steering objective alone sacrifices designs before establishing a consistently larger response. This trade-off motivates using GQT to rank proposals from a structurally informed sequence generator, with structural compatibility constraining which proposals are retained. The TNF comparison identifies the reason to test that strategy; its effect on design yield remains to be assessed.

\subsection{Contact Prediction from Separately Embedded Partners}
\label{sec:jaxka-interface-results}

Backbone-only design pipelines such as inverse folding need predictors that can assess candidate interactions from backbone geometry and sequence. As with $\mathrm{p}K_a$ prediction, the aim was to provide a readout compatible with these inputs. Predicting contacts from separately embedded partners would also allow candidate interactions to be screened without first constructing a complex pose. We therefore tested whether these representations retained interface information and whether stronger contact supervision also improved binding-shift prediction. PINDER supplied an initial test in which cross-chain edges were removed but each partner retained its bound conformation.

% Source: kb/experiments/jaxka-edge-binder.md, "Run 4, hotspot masking and dRSA";
% figures/jaxka-pinder-recipe-comparison/plotted-data.csv.
The baseline multi-task model, run4, recovered contact information from the separated representations, with contact average precision about twice the contact prevalence (0.0291 against 0.0138). Within-complex AUROC was 0.6053 (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-recipe-comparison}\,(A)). This metric measures discrimination between contacting and noncontacting residue pairs within the same complex, so differences between complexes cannot account for the ranking. Local interface information was therefore retained after separation, although contact precision remained low.

Because contact precision remained low, we added within-complex ranking and PeSTo-guided sequence masking in the hmask recipe. Inference used complete sequences and required no PeSTo calculation. Comparing three-seed hmask means with the single run4 seed, within-complex AUROC increased from 0.6053 to 0.6575 and contact average precision from 0.0291 to 0.0406. Whereas within-complex AUROC measures contact discrimination for supplied partners, swapped-partner accuracy tests whether the supplied partner scores above an alternative protein. Mean antibody/antigen swapped-partner accuracy increased from 0.6341 to 0.6656 against same-stratum alternatives (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-recipe-comparison}\,(A, B)). Ranking and masking changed together, so this comparison measures their combined effect.

Distance-based ranking did not distinguish contacts by how much surface they buried. The dRSA recipe introduced this distinction while retaining hmask augmentation. Mean surface-interface AUROC, which measures interface-residue discrimination among surface residues using each residue's highest contact score, increased from 0.6598 under hmask to 0.7004 under dRSA. Contact average precision increased from 0.0406 to 0.0458 and antibody/antigen swapped-partner accuracy from 0.6656 to 0.7039. Surface-interface and swapped-partner improvements occurred in every seed (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-recipe-comparison}\,(A, B)). Distance-defined within-complex AUROC instead decreased slightly, from 0.6575 to 0.6546. Burial weighting therefore favoured contacts that buried exposed surface without improving all proximity-based discrimination.

At checkpoints selected by state and interface binding-shift error, the contact gains did not produce a consistent improvement in binding-shift MAE. On the fixed evaluation set excluding disulfide-bonded cysteines, mean binding-induced $\Delta\mathrm{p}K_a$ MAE changed from 0.06561 under hmask to 0.06608 under dRSA, with mixed changes across seeds (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-recipe-comparison}\,(C)). These contact gains were measured with bound conformations retained. Using the scores for screening therefore required tests of partner discrimination under different negative constructions and transfer to independently folded inputs.

\subsection{Negative Construction and Geometric Consistency}
\label{sec:jaxka-negative-geometry-results}

% Sources: kb/experiments/jaxka-edge-binder.md, negative construction and ANM
% consistency; kb/experiments/jaxka-negatome.md, matched preparation controls.
Partner discrimination depends on how negatives are constructed. Changing interface residue identities tests a synthetic sequence contrast, while replacing the partner tests discrimination against an assumed nonbinder. Noncontacting residues from the observed partner instead provide structural negatives within the supplied pose. We compared these constructions to determine whether improvements in ranking extended to local contact discrimination and antibody/antigen recognition.

Training with retyped and batch-swapped decoys improved overall swapped-partner accuracy but reduced antibody/antigen discrimination. Relative to run4, same-stratum accuracy increased from 0.731 to 0.826, whereas antibody/antigen accuracy fell from 0.634 to 0.566 (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-ablations}\,(A)). Discrimination between partners sharing a UniProt identity remained near chance. The aggregate gain therefore did not establish more specific partner recognition. Within-complex ranking improved local contact discrimination, with AUROC increasing from 0.605 under run4 to 0.664 and 0.687 at ranking coefficients of 0.3 and 1.0, respectively. The larger coefficient also increased state-plus-interface $\mathrm{p}K_a$ MAE. We retained coefficient 0.3 and removed retyped-decoy training in hmask. These early ranking variants still included retyped decoys, so their comparison with hmask does not isolate the ranking term.

Because swapped partners were assumed negatives, we also tested supervision from Negatome-listed non-interactions.\supercite{blohmNegatomeDatabaseNoninteracting2014} This introduced curated negative evidence, but exposed a dependence on structure preparation. Observed partners were initially represented by AlphaFold models cropped to the crystal-observed residues, whereas Negatome non-partners used full-length models. On the same 74 complexes, the original Negatome-trained model's accuracy fell from 0.881 to 0.550 when the observed partners were also supplied at full length. In a separate control containing 418 complexes, the model preferred the cropped version of the same partner in 95.0\% of cases. Differences in preparation therefore accounted for part of the apparent partner discrimination (\cref{app:jaxka-negatome-comparisons}).

Matching preparation during training gave limited gains that did not transfer consistently. With full-length AlphaFold models for both candidate partners, the recipe combining ordinary free-branch AlphaFold augmentation with matched Negatome supervision reached accuracy 0.587 on 95 complexes, against 0.475 for the within-ranking control. Antibody/antigen swap accuracy on AlphaFold inputs nevertheless fell from 0.638 to 0.593 (\cref{tab:jaxka-negatome-comparisons}). All recipes used one seed, and the standard error of each Negatome accuracy was approximately 0.051. On the paired 74-complex subset used for the preparation control, the matched recipe also failed to improve on the original Negatome-trained model (0.529 against 0.550). Matching preparation removed a confound in the comparison, but did not yield a generally better partner-ranking model.

The preparation effect also motivated testing sensitivity to changes in the supplied coordinates. We augmented hmask training with minimised structures and anisotropic network model (ANM) perturbations, then penalised differences in free-state contact logits and $\mathrm{p}K_a$ predictions between alternative and crystal-derived views. Across three seeds, consistency training reduced the increase in state-plus-interface MAE on approximately 2~\AA{} perturbations from 0.038--0.040 to 0.023--0.026, evaluated on the same 793 complexes (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-ablations}\,(C)). Crystal-conformation antibody/antigen swap accuracy increased in every seed, while overall swap accuracy decreased in every seed (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-ablations}\,(B)). Consistency training therefore reduced sensitivity to these perturbations without consistently improving partner discrimination. The comparison assumes that the training labels remain applicable across nearby views; it does not measure experimental $\mathrm{p}K_a$ changes between conformations.

Restricting alternative geometries to the contact losses did not resolve this trade-off. Neither edge-only augmentation nor edge-only consistency improved the seed-17 antibody/antigen result over hmask. Adding edge-only augmentation to dRSA reduced antibody/antigen swap accuracy from 0.702 to 0.668, with little change in burial-weighted contact ranking (\cref{app:jaxka-geometry-comparisons}). We therefore retained hmask and dRSA without ANM augmentation for the POISK comparison, which tested whether their contact readouts transferred to independently folded partners.

\subsection{POISK Binder/Decoy Discrimination}
\label{sec:jaxka-poisk-results}

% Sources: kb/experiments/028-poisk-free-monomer.md;
% figures/jaxka-poisk-recipe-comparison/plotted_data.csv; matched Pegasus readouts.
POISK tested whether the contact readouts developed on PINDER could distinguish antibody binders from matched decoys. Each benchmark pair contains a binder and a sequence-similar decoy against the same antigen. We compared three forms of structural input, using independently folded ABodyBuilder2 Fvs and ESMFold antigens, partners extracted from Protenix-v2 complexes and embedded separately, or the complete supplied complex (\cref{fig:jaxka-poisk-recipe-comparison}). The second comparison retains each partner's predicted bound conformation while removing its relative placement. Pairwise accuracy measures how often the binder scores above its matched decoy, with chance at 0.5.

Hmask and dRSA improved discrimination in both separately embedded input conditions. On 11,111 independently folded pairs, accuracy using the mean of the top-50 CDR contact probabilities increased from 0.437 under run4 to 0.712 under hmask and 0.713 under dRSA. The corresponding hmask and dRSA accuracies using separated bound conformers were 0.693 and 0.700. Predicted-burial weighting also improved the independently folded comparison, from 0.490 under run4 to 0.667 under hmask and 0.675 under dRSA, although the top-50 CDR mean remained the stronger readout. These gains did not require the supplied complex pose.

Supplying the complete complex did not produce the same improvement. Top-50 CDR contact accuracy remained between 0.509 and 0.523 across the three recipes, while predicted antigen burial change gave accuracies of 0.575, 0.575 and 0.565 for run4, hmask and dRSA. The strongest binder/decoy discrimination in this comparison therefore came from the separately embedded representations. Whether it required recognition of the supplied antigen remained unresolved.

To determine whether the improved rankings required the correct antigen, we repeated the independently folded comparison after replacing it. Burial-weighted CDR accuracy changed from 0.667 to 0.665 under hmask and from 0.675 to 0.666 under dRSA (\cref{tab:jaxka-poisk-wrong-antigen}). Most of the discrimination survived antigen replacement. Separate learned readouts on pooled GQT embeddings showed the same behaviour. For dRSA, antibody-only, correct-antigen and wrong-antigen accuracies were 0.897, 0.893 and 0.896, respectively (\cref{tab:jaxka-poisk-learned-controls}). No decoy antibody appeared as a binder in another pair, so antibody features alone could distinguish the two pools. Antigen-cluster holdout did not remove this shortcut.

Because pool membership could support discrimination without partner recognition, we next held each binder fixed and ranked its own antigen against alternatives from different antigen clusters. Antibody-only features cancel within this comparison. Across five splits, trained readouts on elementwise antibody--antigen embedding products gave hmask accuracy of $0.562 \pm 0.013$, compared with $0.535 \pm 0.011$ for dRSA and $0.532 \pm 0.016$ for sequence-composition products (\cref{tab:jaxka-poisk-binders-only}). Hmask retained modest pairing discrimination under this readout, while dRSA remained close to the composition control. Its stronger binder/decoy discrimination did not translate into better antigen pairing.

Using these rankings for screening therefore requires a separate assessment of the proposed partner interaction. In the KRAS pMHC application, we used hmask scores to prioritise candidates, then compared G12D, WT and peptide-free target contexts and predicted complexes to investigate their structural compatibility (\cref{sec:jaxka-kras-results}).

\subsection{KRAS pMHC Screening}
\label{sec:jaxka-kras-results}

% Sources: Poole et al. (2022), p. 3; Anthropic/Adaptyv Challenge 5 brief.
Mutation-specific recognition is difficult when the altered side chain faces into the MHC groove. In the KRAS G12D ten-residue peptide \texttt{VVVGADGVGK} presented by HLA-A*11:01, the mutation occupies peptide position P6. In the TCR-bound structure, the Asp6 side chain is buried within the groove, where it interacts with the HLA molecule.\supercite{pooleTherapeuticHighAffinity2022} Its orientation limits direct access to the chemical difference between mutant and WT, making the surrounding peptide--MHC surface part of the recognition problem.

This recognition problem formed Challenge 5 of the 2026 Anthropic $\times$ Adaptyv Bio Protein Design Competition. The task required binding to KRAS G12D pMHC while avoiding both the corresponding WT complex and peptide-free MHC.\supercite{anthropicAdaptyvKRASChallenge2026} The WT comparison tests discrimination of the mutation, while the peptide-free comparison tests whether recognition depends on the presented peptide. These requirements supplied the matched target contexts for assessing candidates from the blind screen.

% Source: kb/experiments/046-atlas-kras-screen.md, experiments 045/046/055.
We screened 69,060 ATLAS interface representatives using the hmask checkpoint, in an application that preceded dRSA training. Retaining the fragments' source coordinates and residue-level target assignments allowed the proposed contacts to be examined in subsequent complex predictions. The separately embedded target supplied candidate contact sites without requiring a complex pose. Library reduction and subsequent selection are described in \cref{app:jaxka-kras-screening}.

Among the first 100 ranked source chains, three candidates passed the matched confidence criteria across G12D, WT and peptide-free contexts (\cref{tab:jaxka-kras-candidates}). The strongest candidate, \texttt{ID\_1457932}, reached binder--target interface iPTM of 0.875 with G12D pMHC, compared with 0.761 for WT. Restricting the comparison to the shared HLA and $\beta_2$-microglobulin chains gave interface iPTM of 0.854 in the G12D complex and 0.192 in the peptide-free complex. Locating the predicted contacts was then necessary to assess whether this confidence contrast accompanied an interaction near the mutation.

The predicted G12D interface contacted peptide position P6 through four candidate residues, compared with one in the WT model (\cref{fig:jaxka-kras-pmhc-radial}). Two of the G12D-contacting residues belonged to the original screened patch, connecting the blind fragment priority with the subsequent complex prediction. Contact counts use all heavy atoms of P6 and do not by themselves establish direct recognition of the buried Asp side chain.

\subsection{Scaling and Implications}
\label{sec:jaxka-scaling-results}

% Sources: kb/experiments/jaxka-edge-binder.md, Width and relational-edge
% scaling and Data scaling; figures/jaxka-pinder-ablations/inputs/.
Increasing model capacity improved contact recovery without consistently improving partner discrimination. At 50\% data, increasing the dRSA model's width from 64 to 128 raised mean contact AP from 0.0458 to 0.0505, while antibody/antigen swap accuracy fell from 0.7039 to 0.6806 across three seeds (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-ablations}\,(D)). Increasing the number of relation maps also failed to improve antibody/antigen accuracy over the width-64, maps-4/1 model. Greater capacity therefore changed local contact recovery and partner ranking differently. Choosing a model for screening requires evaluating the partner comparison it will be used to make.

More training data extended the wider model's contact gain to antibody/antigen discrimination in the seed-17 comparison. Under the 20-epoch schedule, increasing the training pool from 50\% to 100\% raised width-128 contact AP from 0.0536 to 0.0619 and antibody/antigen swap accuracy from 0.6791 to 0.7147 (\cref{fig:jaxka-pinder-ablations}\,(E, F)). Width 64 also improved contact AP, from 0.0463 to 0.0549, but its antibody/antigen accuracy fell from 0.7023 to 0.6822. The wider model benefited on both measures under this schedule, supporting further investigation of capacity together with data availability. Replication across seeds is needed to determine whether this joint gain persists.

Extending the full-data training schedule did not improve the reported objectives consistently. With a 40-epoch budget, contact AP fell at both widths, while antibody/antigen accuracy rose to 0.7054 for width 64 and fell to 0.7054 for width 128. State-plus-interface $\mathrm{p}K_a$ MAE increased for both models. The longer schedule also stretched learning-rate decay and doubled stopping patience, so this comparison changes the optimisation trajectory as well as the epoch budget. Checkpoints remained selected by state-plus-interface MAE; width 64 selected epoch 15 after stopping at epoch 31, while width 128 selected epoch 34. These results motivate comparing checkpoint selection by $\mathrm{p}K_a$ error with selection by partner-ranking performance when screening is the intended application.

Further scaling should therefore be assessed against the interaction that screening is intended to identify. The POISK controls showed why binder/decoy discrimination alone was insufficient (\cref{sec:jaxka-poisk-results}), while the KRAS application used matched target contexts and complex predictions to investigate candidates prioritised by the blind scores (\cref{sec:jaxka-kras-results}). A larger model should be retained for screening when its gains extend to partner-dependent ranking under these controls. Prospective enrichment then requires a comparison of ranked and randomly selected candidates under the same prediction or experimental budget.
