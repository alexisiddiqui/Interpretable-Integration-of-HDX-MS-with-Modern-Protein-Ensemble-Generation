Chapter 1

Contextualisation

OMPUTATIONAL chemistry is a branch of science that employs computa-
tional power and the knowledge of physical laws to provide insight about
microscopic phenomena. Despite the difficulties (including the abused

commentaries from Paul Dirac [1] and Walter Kohn [2]), computational chemistry
is applied almost routinely in a number of fields, and its capacities are to such an
extent that a recent perspective considers the problem of “predicting accurately the
properties of a hypothetical polyatomic molecule”, posed by Léwdin in 1967 [3], solved

for most practical purposes [4].

However, the realm of computational molecular biology still poses several prob-
lems of paramount interest both technical and practical. This includes long un-
solved problems, such as protein [5] and RNA [6] folding, but also the modelling of
intrinsically disordered proteins [7] or ligand-receptor interactions [8]. In these sys-
tems, instead of methods based on quantum chemistry, the energy surfaces are ex-
pressed as simplified potentials that attempt to represent physical insight by means

of easily computable functions.
