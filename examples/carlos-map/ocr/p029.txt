In their original article, Born and Oppenheimer showed the feasibility of ne-
glecting coupling terms by means of a cumbersome expansion of the electron-nucleus
mass ratio [34, 35], which will not be reproduced here. We note that there are also
numerous systems where the adiabatic approximations fails because nuclear mo-

tion involves more than one Born-Oppenheimer potential energy surface

These non-adiabatic phenomena have been known for almost a century; an
early paper by Fritz London shows how simple processes, like the trivial charge
transfer Ar* + N —> Ar + N* cannot be accounted from an adiabatic point of
view [36]. Several systems of enormous scientific and technical interest cannot be
studied fully within Born-Oppenheimer approximation, including the vibrations in
graphene [37] or the photodynamics in biological systems [38], among others [39].

The validity of Born-Oppenheimer approximation will be assumed to hold true
for the rest of this thesis. However, even if the nuclei can be assumed fixed, there is
still a many-body term in the molecular Hamiltonian eq. 2.2: the sum of electron-
electron interactions. The treatment of this term and the obtention of electronic
wavefunctions is a very interesting problem by itself that will not be treated in
detail here; a comprehensive overview of the most common methodologies may
be found in [40]. We will assume these methodologies to belong to the working

know-how of computational chemistry.

2.1.2 Ab initio molecular dynamics

Our treatment of Born-Oppenheimer approximation has allowed to separate the
nuclear and electronic degrees of freedom, but the nuclear dynamics are still de-
scribed by a wavefunction. Nevertheless, it turns out that assuming that the nuclei
move classically is a very neat approximation, that gives rise to the fertile terrain of

ab initio molecular dynamics.
