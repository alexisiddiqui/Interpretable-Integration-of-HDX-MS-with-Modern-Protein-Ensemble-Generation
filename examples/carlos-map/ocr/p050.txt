2.3 Polarisable force fields

2.3.1 The need for polarisation

The theory of intermolecular forces that we have just analysed leaves classical force
fields in a quite unfortunate position. From the three interactions obtained from
second-order perturbation theory, the electrostatics are modelled crudely (the charge
is merely the first-order term in a Taylor expansion of the energy), the induction is

absent, and the dispersion is simplified to a Lennard-Jones potential.

The accuracy of first-generation (also termed additive) force fields is quite limited
by this simplistic functional form. There is a significant body of evidence suggesting
that an inaccurate modelling of electronic distribution is the main source of errors,

regardless of imprecision in the valence and dispersion potentials [95].

A commonly echoed argument states that a single water molecule presents a
dipole moment of 1.85 D in gas phase [96, 97], but this increases to approximately
2.1 D in the dimer, further in large water clusters [98], and about 2.95 D in bulk
[99, 100]. However, classical MD simulations of the latter describe an average dipole
of 2.4-2.6 D [101, 102].
