Initial studies assessed the performance of multilayer perceptrons, a type of neu-
ral networks, for predicting multipolar moments for hydrogen fluoride [201], water
[202, 203] and shortly after glycine and N-methylacetamide [19]. However, after a
comparative study of several machine learning methods, including multilayer per-
ceptrons, radial basis function neural networks and kriging [204], it was decided
that the latter was the most adequate. Subsequent work showed that kriging out-
performed artificial neural networks in water [205], ethanol [206], alanine [207],
histidine [208] and serine [209].

Later on, after testing the multipole model in rigid body simulations of imida-
zole [210], and the electrostatic potential of crambin [181], the current structure of
FFLUX was developed in a paper by Mills and Popelier including the explicit for-
mulas for the electrostatic forces [206].

The last years have seen several improvements in the basics of the method, that
include the improvement of the sampling method [211], progresses in the featuriza-
tion [212] and the development of a highly parallelized kriging engine [196, 213].
The prediction of multipoles has also been tested in several systems of larger size,
including cholesterol [214], carbohydrates [215], aminoacids [216] and small pep-
tides [217].

Finally, during last year, the work from Popelier group has been applied to the
geometry optimisation of a single water molecule [218] and peptide-capped glycine
[219], which sets a start for the upscaling of the project.
