A numerical integration of the full Schrodinger equation is possible for small
systems, although usually performed by means of wavepacket methods [25, 26].
This has been done to study several simple reactions [27], although bigger systems
can be approached using simulations based on Feynman’s path integral formula-
tion [28]. We note that these methodologies are necessary when the nuclear quan-
tum effects are of importance, for example in tunnelling, which is known to be of

importance in DNA mutation [29] and in the mechanism of many enzymes [30]

Nevertheless, the most extended approach to tackle equation 2.2 is the Born-
Oppenheimer approximation, that separates the nuclear and electronic degrees of
freedom, effectively reducing the three many-body problems to one. Most quan-
tum chemistry textbooks, including [31] and [32], introduce the Born-Oppenheimer
approximation in a very simplistic manner, and even graduate-level texts such as
[33] provide a reduced approach. Given the importance of this approach, espe-
cially in the connection of practical molecular dynamics with quantum mechanics,

we provide a full derivation.
