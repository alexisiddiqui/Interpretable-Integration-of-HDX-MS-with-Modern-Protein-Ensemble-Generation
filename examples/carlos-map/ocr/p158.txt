Let us consider the following example: take an arbitrary group G of order h,
and define a representation consisting of the identity matrix repeated h times. This
fulfils the definition of a group completely, but is a weak tool if we are trying to

“represent” the elements of the group with matrices.

We are then interested in a very particular kind of representation: a set of ma-
trices where every matrix behaves as one element of the group. In a slightly more
mathematical terminology, we are looking for an isomorphism, a one-to-one map-
ping between the group G and a subgroup of GLn(R). The result of this mapping

is known as a faithful representation:

Definition 4 (Faithful representation) Given a finite group G of order h, a faithful rep-
resentation of G is a set of h invertible N x N matrices that form a group K isomorphic to
G. This is, to every element X € G there is one, and only one element Y € K that operates

in the same way.

It remains to wonder what kinds of groups have faithful representations. Fortu-
nately, it turns out that an arbitrary group has, at least, one faithful representation.
A basic result in finite group theory, Cayley’s theorem, ensures that any finite group
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group Sy, the group containing the
permutations of N elements. Since any permutation can be represented faithfully
by an N x N permutation matrix, then any group will at least have the faithful

representation of the subgroup of Sy to which it is isomorphic [325].

Due to the existence of faithful representations, we can express the symmetry
operations of a group in terms of matrices. In fact, from now on we will assume
that the term representation means a faithful representation that has been brought to
a form where it can act on the Cartesian coordinates of a molecule expressed as a

vector of the form:
