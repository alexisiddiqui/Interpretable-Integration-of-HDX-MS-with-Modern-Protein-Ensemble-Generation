2.1 Molecular dynamics
2.1.1 Time-dependent Schrédinger equation

The realm of matter at the microscopic level is governed by a set of complicated,
and somehow perplexing rules, dictated by quantum mechanics. This concerns
mainly the world at dimensions close to 10~° m, although quantum effects have
been observed at much larger scales [22]. Any computational methodology that
aims to faithfully reflect the behaviour of matter at this level must be (to a certain

extent, as we will see) consistent with the laws of quantum mechanics.

In practice, quantum mechanics is not a scientific theory per se, but rather a frame
theory: a paradigm that includes several theories with slightly different conditions
of applicability. These theories constitute the most powerful and consistent phys-
ical framework to understand microscopic phenomena. However, we should also
note that quantum theory is still in development: for example, recent high-accuracy
measurements in the Hz molecule show that even the most accurate quantum cal-

culations still fail to correlate with the dissociation energy up to 10~? cm™! [23].

Fortunately, the systems treated in this thesis will fulfil two conditions that allow
to select the most appropriate theory. In the first place, the scales of energy will be
relatively low, around a few electron-volts; therefore, quantum field theory is not
required. In the second place, effects due to relativity will be mostly negligible, with
the only exception of spin. Accordingly, we can employ non-relativistic quantum

mechanics, where spin is treated in an ad hoc manner.
