Before proceeding to the exposition, it is valuable to reflect on why is it interest-
ing to study a bond order indicator. The answer can be summarised in a sentence:
although theoretical chemistry is traditionally in charge of developing concepts that
can make sense of chemical phenomena, one of such concepts, the chemical bond,
is still a significant matter of debate in the literature. The reasons of this conflict are

both historical and technical, and deserve to be considered carefully.

The chemical bond is traditionally understood in terms of the formulation pro-
posed by Gilbert N. Lewis in 1916 [250], that perceives a chemical bond as a pair
of electrons that is shared between two atoms. This vision was, however, brought
forth before the general advent of quantum mechanics, and reconciling the early
vision of shared electron pairs with wavefunctions has been a classical problem in

theoretical and computational chemistry.

Most of the attempts to reconcile both pictures have passed through electron-
counting arguments, such as those familiar from introductory chemistry. In those,
bond order is identified as the difference of electrons localized in bonding and an-
tibonding orbitals. However, this concept is of little use in many ordinary appli-
cations of quantum chemistry, for example when a single electron configuration is
a poor approximation to the electronic structure, or in periodic systems, where the

plane wave nature of the basis functions challenges the orbital interpretation.

Although many attempts have been made to modernise these arguments [251,
252], there is to date no universally accepted bond order indicator. In a way, the
theoretical object introduced in this chapter attempts to reconcile Lewis’ picture
with modern electronic structure in a different way, by recognising the concept of a
pair of delocalized electrons within the wavefunction, employing the formalism of

Quantum Chemical Topology presented in Section 2.4.
