We note that both Keesom and Debye assumed molecules with non-zero dipolar

However, both Keesom and Debye contributions are still incomplete. In the
first place, there is no way to explain the van der Waals interactions in the noble
gases, whose electronic densities are exactly spherically symmetric. On the other
hand, in the liquid and solid states, arguments of symmetry should lead to a great

diminishing, if not cancellation, of the aforementioned forces.

These problems were recognised by Fritz London in his seminal 1930 paper [79]
[80], which presented a novel contribution to the intermolecular forces, the dis-
persion®, with a completely quantum-mechanical origin. London introduced these
forces using a toy example: two coupled harmonic oscillators that gave rise to an

attractive force with an asymptotic R-6 decay.

Altogether, these findings comprise the three main types of intermolecular forces:
electrostatics (known as Keesom forces), polarisation (known as Debye forces) and

dispersion (known as London forces).
