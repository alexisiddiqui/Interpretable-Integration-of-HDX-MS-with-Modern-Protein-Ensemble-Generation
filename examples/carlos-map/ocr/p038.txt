The terminology of this field is better understood within a brief historical con-
text. The first insights regarding the presence of intermolecular forces can be traced
back to 1743 [73], where a treaty of hydrostatics points out that capillary action can-
not be explained only by surface interactions between molecules and the tube walls;

actually, it requires a general interaction, embracing all molecules.

The theoretical advances of Johannes Diderik van der Waals, through its cele-
brated state equation (which would award him the Nobel Prize in Physics, in 1910),
transformed the existence of intermolecular forces in a topic of general interest. In

van der Waals’ equation

(P + vr) (V —nb) = nRT (2.37)

the term b corrects the volume by taking into account the intrinsic volume of
the component particles, and a corrects the pressure by means of certain attractive
interactions between the particles (in fact, some authors have defined intermolecu-
lar forces as that force which gives rise to the constant a in van der Waals equation [74]).
In his Nobel Lecture, van der Waals [75] declared that I came to the conclusion that
the attraction of the molecules decreases extremely quickly with distance, indeed that the

attraction only has an appreciable value at distances close to the size of the molecules.

The early theories of intermolecular forces have named the three most impor-
tant contributions to long-range interactions. Initially, Willem H. Keesom theorised
that van der Waals forces between non-charged molecules arise as a consequence of
the electrostatic interaction between their dipoles [76]. Although symmetry seems
to indicate that the global interaction must be zero, Boltzmann statistics assign sta-
tistical preference to low energy configurations, creating an attractive force with
