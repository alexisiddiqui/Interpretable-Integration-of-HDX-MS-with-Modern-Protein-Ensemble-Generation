The only hindrance of our proposed structure is that Lewis’ rules are not ver-
ified. There are two reasons why this should be unimportant. In the first place,
Lewis’ rules arose from the study of compounds stable in the narrow range of ther-
modynamical conditions accessible to the laboratories before 1916. On the other
hand, the molecule of Ng has been found at 40 GPa, six orders of magnitude above

the atmospheric pressure: there is no reason to expect that these rules are universal.

In fact, there are several examples of exotic bonding patterns in this range of
pressures, that include CO adopting unusual coordination [288], CO forming poly-
mers [289]. Even more importantly, N2O suffers a transformation to a -N(NO)N(NO)-
polymer at 60 GPa [290]. If the results of quantum mechanics suggest a novel in-
terpretation of the bonding, perhaps it only means that new insight should be ac-
cepted, instead of resorting to rules that, while useful, are more than a hundred

years old.

A second argument is that pentavalent nitrogen is actually a well-known bond-
ing pattern, present in for example N2O [291]. Moreover, as suggested by Chung
et al [286], Ng can actually be considered as a diimide substituted by two azide
groups. The apparent transgression of Lewis’ rules is actually reasonable by com-

parison with well-known model molecules.
