146 CHAPTER 5. SYMMETRY-ADAPTED KRIGING

More often than not, shrewd arguments based on symmetry can provide signif-
icant insight and avoid computations with very little effort. A clear example is the
use of point symmetry in textbook inorganic chemistry to understand molecular vi-
brations and predict the degeneracy of electronic levels, for example in octahedral
metallic complexes [301]. Less extended is translational symmetry, which is used
widely in materials science and solid state chemistry, although it is considerably

more involved due to the need of handling continuous groups [302].

In this chapter we will be concerned with a different type of symmetry: permu-
tational symmetry, of which point symmetry is a particular case. We will incorporate
permutational invariance into Gaussian process regression, in order to enhance its
predictive power, guarantee the physical consistency of the models, and compress

the latter to reduce the computational cost.

The need for such method is easily understood after evaluating a physical ex-
ample. In Figure 5.1, we display all the minima of the methanol molecule, ob-
tained by permutation of the hydrogen indices in the methyl group. It is evident
that all six configurations have the same observable properties, since they share the
same Hamiltonian; the only difference is that the positions of the different hydrogen

atoms are swapped.

Note that the methanol molecule in its global minimum has C,; symmetry, and
thus has an only element of point symmetry: a plane a defined by the C-O-H atoms.
Except for the permutation of atoms Ha and Hg, none of the other four conforma-
tions can be generated by point symmetry alone: they require the more general
permutational symmetry. In fact, we could also consider the permutation of any of
Ha, Hg or He with the hydroxyl hydrogen. We will however obviate this possi-
bility because the exchange of these atoms occurs only during chemical reactions,

which are beyond the scope of this chapter.
