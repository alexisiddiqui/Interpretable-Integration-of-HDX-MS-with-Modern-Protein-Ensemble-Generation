At first sight, the results show significant disagreement, with an average error
of 600 kcal mol~!. The standard deviation is 4 kcal mol~!, and a rapid comparison
shows that the majority of the predictions are contained in a range of 5 kcal mol!
at both sides of the mean of the Gaussian process, 4 = —455.502467 a. u., almost 1

been validated in [219] for small distortions of the training set, where the maximum

errors were reported to be in the range of 1-2 kcal mol™!.

Moreover, a quick glance to the distribution of energies will reveal that the pre-
dictions of FFLUX are significantly detached from the real values. This is done in
Figure 3.8, where we report the energies as distributions around the mean, in or-
der to make them comparable. It can be seen that, while the test set is basically
a Gaussian distribution around the centre, FFLUX predicts an unrelated bimodal
distribution. More importantly, the minima of energy (which can be observed in
Figure 3.8, as the few examples in the left extreme of the histogram) are not ranked

correctly, but rather their energies are placed randomly within the distribution.

What is happening can be described as follows. In the 51-dimensional space
spanned by the features of capped glycine, the training set containing barely a thou-
sand examples is extremely sparse, and the interpolation of an arbitrary point in this
space yields a senseless result. This is unobserved if one employs a test set that is
very similar to the training set, as is usually done during parametrization tests, but

occurs clearly when one attempts to perform simulation.
