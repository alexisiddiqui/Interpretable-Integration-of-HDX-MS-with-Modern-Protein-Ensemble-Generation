The CCSD values decrease significantly for directly bonded atoms, finding re-
spectively that 6(H,H) = 0.844, 6(O,O) = 1.615 and 5(N,N) = 2.310 for the
molecules presented above. This phenomenon is easily explained since the inclu-
sion of Coulomb correlation means that electrons, on average, repel more strongly

than if only Fermi correlation is included.

The opposite effect is found in weak interactions, since Hartree-Fock, as a mean
field theory, cannot take into account the instantaneous fluctuations that give rise to
the dispersion interactions. Clear examples are Nez and Ar2, where the DI changes
from 6(Ne,Ne) = 0.005 to 6(Ne,Ne) = 0.009, and from 6(Ar,Ar) = 0.002 to
6(Ar,Ar) = 0.015. Perhaps unsurprisingly, hydrogen bonds do not seem to fol-
low this pattern: they remain barely unchanged, which might be an indicator of the

importance of exchange in these interactions.

Ionic compounds show small changes upon inclusion of Coulomb correlation.
In the model LiH and NaCl molecules, we obtain a observe a change from 6(Li, H) =
0.193 to 6(Li,H) = 0.199 and from 6(Na,Cl) = 0.193 to 6(Na,Cl) = 0.222. The
small changes in geometry are negligible, and comparisons of DIs obtained with
the same geometry yield an identical result. Although the change in DI upon in-
clusion of electron correlation is small in these systems, it is rather clear that the DI

increases, like in the weak-interaction limit.

Finally, we note that delocalization indices obtained at the CCSD/Miiller level
are highly correlated with Hartree-Fock values: Pearson’s correlation coefficient for
the 1,666 bonds analysed is 0.938, and the individual correlation coefficients for
each group of compounds are all above 0.9.

As the calculated wavefunction approaches the exact wavefunction, our inter-
pretation of it evolves. In view of these results, Lewis structures can be considered
as a baseline, and the deviations of it at higher levels of theory are not a failure to
recover bond order, but contrarily offer a deeper understanding into the physical
nature of bonding. The delocalization index thus provides a tool that extends the

classical idea of bond order towards general domains.
