Regarding the first response, it must be kept in mind that delocalization indices
measure the number of electron pairs shared between two atoms. As a first con-
sequence, apolar bonds will in general return larger DI values, since the electrons
are equally shared. By the contrary, in a polar bond, the most electronegative atom
hoards the electrons and the equal sharing is less favourable. Following equation
4.8, the most electronegative atom increases its localization index at the expense of

the delocalization index.

An useful example is posed by N2 and HCN, molecules that have a triple bond
in their Lewis structures. The respective delocalization indices computed at the
HF level show 6(N,N) = 3.043 for N2 and 6(C,N) = 2.340 for HCN, while the
localization indices are A(N) = 5.479 in N2 and A(N) = 7.141 and A(C) = 3.202
in HCN. This demonstrates the localization of electrons in the nitrogen, the most

electronegative atom, decreasing the DI.

Regarding the influence of the environment, delocalization indices can recover
two ubiquitous phenomena: the inductive and resonance effects. The first is the ac-
tion of a charged group (or one with a significant electronegativity), that withdraws
electron density from a given bond, altering its chemical behaviour. The second ap-
pears in the form of electron delocalization, for example by reducing or enhancing

the double bond character in a conjugated system.

An example of the inductive effect can be found in homologous series. In Figure
4.3 we display how the delocalization index of the C-O bond varies across the series
of alcohols and aldehydes. Since carbon is more electronegative than hydrogen, it
exerts a small polarisation effect on the carbon-oxygen bond; however, this effect is
highly local, and converges readily to a fixed value as the length of the carbonated
chain increases. Note that we are not attempting to explain the order of acidity of
the alcohols, which would require considering additional factors such as enthalpies
of solvation; this is merely an exploration of the effects of inductive effects in neu-

tral, gas phase molecules.

Similarly, the delocalization index can recover the effects traditionally explained
by means of standard arrow-pushing rules. A good example is posed by benzene

derivatives, where the substituents play a major role in the reactivity and structure.
