However, we have shown a trivial counterexample that maximum likelihood
estimation is not necessarily correct for a finite number of datapoints. Solutions like
Bessel’s correction are in general not that trivial, and usually empirical knowledge

determines the validity of MLE.

A second caveat is perhaps more subtle. The above mentioned theorem ensures
that MLE can yield the true parameter for the distribution f(x; 6), but nothing has
been said about that distribution being correct. If one tries to model a parabola of
points with a linear model, although the estimated parameters will be the “true”
values for the equation of the line, the model will not fit the data at all.

In the case of kriging, this mostly refers to the selection of an adequate model of
the covariance. If the selected kernel does not adequately reflect the correlation, the

parameters obtained by maximum likelihood estimation will be quite poor.

The third caveat is of technical nature: maximum likelihood estimation only
works for the global maximum of the likelihood, and in general this cannot be done
analytically. Moreover, from equation 2.159 it follows that the inverse of the co-
variance matrix K has to be computed; this requires a Cholesky decomposition that

scales as O(N?), which can result in enormous computational expenses.

The field of global optimisation is a rich area by itself. In the following subsec-
tion we discuss some of the algorithms that can be employed for maximum likeli-

hood estimation.

We note that there are other methods beyond MLE to obtain the hyperparam-
eters of a kriging model [190], mostly of Bayesian nature, although those require
usually operations with prohibitive computational expenses, such as the evalua-

tion of very high-dimensional integrals by Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms.
