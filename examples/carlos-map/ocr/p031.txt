Equation 2.20 defines the basis of Born-Oppenheimer molecular dynamics, where
the forces on each atom are computed from an ab initio calculation. This method is
compatible with virtually every electronic structure method (even when analyti-
cal derivatives are not available, since these can always be computed numerically,
although this is hardly advisable for most practical cases due to the strong compu-
tational overhead), and has been applied in different contexts, from solvation [43]

to reactivity [44].

However, since the errors in the derivatives are approximately linear on the
wavefunction error, BOMD simulations require a rather tight convergence criterion
to produce meaningful forces [45], resulting in significant computational expenses.
It seems that this problem could be palliated if the wavefunction from the previous
step is used as guess, although this results in drifts in energy [46] due to the break-
ing of time-reversal symmetry. Some methods have been proposed to reduce this
problem [47, 48, 49], but their impact in the literature is rather recent, and small in

comparison with its alternative: Car-Parrinello molecular dynamics.

This approach was proposed by Robert Car and Michele Parrinello in 1985 [50],
and allows to avoid the tight convergence of the wavefunction while maintaining
most of the advantages of Born-Oppenheimer molecular dynamics. The importance
of CPMD is such that no modern treatment of ab initio molecular dynamics can be
complete in absence of even a brief analysis. In this line of thought, although we
