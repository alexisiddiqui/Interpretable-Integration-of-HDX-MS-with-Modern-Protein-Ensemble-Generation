In this way, as ||r|| — 0, the prediction tends to yz, which is a constant value,
and thus the derivatives will be null. Therefore, if a given molecule has not been
sampled properly, optimisation becomes impossible. This problem is similar to the
catastrophic lengthening of bonds that is observed in classical force fields, when

anharmonic corrections that include terms up to cubic are used [63].

In practice, we observe that the extrapolation collapse occurs rather soon. An
example is displayed in Figure 3.4 for the dissociation curve of water employing
a state-of-the-art, well-tested model. We observe that the potential is very realistic
around the minimum, where the sampling points concentrate, but soon the O-H
distance is longer than any example in the training set and the potential becomes

flat.

The extrapolation collapse threatens the application of FFLUX beyond toy prob-
lems: for even moderate systems (due to the curse of dimensionality analysed in next
section), for example ethanol or N-methylacetamide, optimisations easily get stuck
in flat surfaces, yielding unreasonable results. The problem is even more acute

when attempting finite-temperature simulations, as we will show.
