4.2.4 The stability of classical patterns

The magnitude of our study, having built a database of ~ 200 compounds (account-
ing for 14,756 entries of delocalization indices, only for Hartree-Fock calculations)
allows to apply several data mining techniques to extract trends from the large num-
bers of data points. In this section we perform some of such analyses, which have
focused on the most abundant atom pairs in the database: carbon-carbon (2,364
data points), carbon-oxygen (632 data points), carbon-nitrogen (724 data points)
and carbon-hydrogen interactions (5,497 data points).

In Figure 4.1, we display the spread of the delocalization indices for several
classes of bonds. The first realisation is that there is a sharp discontinuity between
the weak interactions, that are below ~ 0.5 and the rest, signalling the existence of

chemical bonds.

More importantly, it is clear that there are several plateaus, that under close ex-
amination reveal to be stable well-known bond orders. For example, in the carbon-
carbon plot (upper left panel) there is a wide plateau around 1.0 that contains many
bonds that are recognisable as single, such as the C-C bonds of hydrocarbons. The
other two plateaus can easily be identified with an aromatic C-C bond (plateau at
1.4, containing for example conjugated bonds from benzene, thiophene and pyrrole
and their derivatives) and a double C=C bond (plateau at 1.8, containing the double

bonds in alkenes, etc.).

Some points in the extreme of the same plot correspond to triple carbon-carbon
bonds, but these are underrepresented in our study, both because of their scarcity
and due to the well-known formation of a non-nuclear maximum in triple bonds
[144], that entails difficulties in the analysis. It is likely that a similar plateau emerges

if enough triple bond-containing compounds are added.

Figure 4.1, there are three plateaus. Firstly, the plateau around 0.8 comprises single
carbon-oxygen bonds, such as the founds in alcohols and carboxylic acids. The
