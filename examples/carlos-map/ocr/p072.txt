2.5 Machine learning

The term machine learning was supposedly coined by Arthur L. Samuel in the
fifties, as a paradigm in which computers can learn to perform tasks without being ex-
plicitly programmed [184]. This branch of statistics and computer science, which is
rather undefined due to the enormous advancements in the last few years, com-
prises a large number of techniques, mathematical and computational, sprouted

from different areas.

Aside from recent developments, arising from the so-called deep learning revolu-
tion [16] that uses very specialised neural networks to solve a wide range of prob-
lems, the impact of machine learning in the scientific community is mostly related
to non-parametric statistical regression models. Despite the awe of the name, most
of these approaches are actually quite simple, being generalisations of well-known
statistical models, such as least squares regression or simple interpolation methods.
For example, kriging can be shown to be a generalisation of interpolation by splines
[185].

In this section, we will introduce Gaussian Process regression (also known as
kriging), a very powerful non-parametric regression method that is employed by

the Quantum Chemical Topology Force Field to build its potential.

2.5.1 Gaussian process regression

Gaussian process regression is one of the most powerful techniques in machine
learning. Its origin is usually linked to geostatistics, where it is used for ore predic-
tion [186], although we note that its use can actually be traced back to Laplace. This
technique received special attention in the nineties, when it was used by Sacks et al
to analyse the output of computer simulations [187]. Nowadays, it is mostly used

in Bayesian optimisation [188].
