## Argument

Under matched exchange conditions, an [[HDX-MS]] uptake change identifies altered protection but does not identify the structural event responsible. Increased exposure, loss of hydrogen bonding or changes in conformational populations can contribute to the observation. Even residue-resolved exchange does not directly distinguish alternative structural interpretations ([[@englanderHydrogenExchangeModern1997#^c3]]), and an HDX peptide set need not contain enough information for a clear structural interpretation ([[@bradshawInterpretationHDXData2020#^c8]]).

## Caveats

The protection comparison assumes matched intrinsic exchange chemistry and labelling times ([[intrinsic exchange rate]]). Englander's example is native-state HX of cytochrome c measured by NMR; peptide averaging in HDX-MS adds ambiguity.

### Historical title

> A change in HDX-MS uptake shows a change in protection but does not on its own identify the structural mechanism.

### Historical argument

> Englander and Krishna state that measured exchange does not directly distinguish between alternative structural interpretations, even with residue-resolved data. Masson et al. (2019) attribute the difficulty of interpretation to the complexity of the exchange mechanism. Bradshaw et al. state that HDX data reflect an ensemble that may not be explained by a single state, and that there is no guarantee a peptide set holds enough information for a clear structural interpretation.

### Historical caveats

> Englander's example is native-state HX of cytochrome c measured by NMR, not peptide-level HDX-MS; peptide averaging only adds ambiguity.
