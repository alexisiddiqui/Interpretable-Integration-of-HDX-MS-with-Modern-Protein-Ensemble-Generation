## Definition

Allostery is the mechanism by which chemical information is propagated between spatially distinct sites within a protein, so binding at one site changes activity at another. Enzymes use dynamic allosteric pathways to transmit long-range binding information and control catalysis. In some systems, such as IGPS, activation is driven by millisecond motions without any large structural rearrangement.

## Why it matters here

Allostery is the standard example of function encoded in [[protein dynamics]] and [[population shift|state populations]] rather than in a single structure. Section 1.1 uses it to argue that static structures miss mechanisms.

Where allostery acts through populations, a weighted ensemble can show the shift. Where it acts through millisecond dynamics without large rearrangement, as in IGPS, populations alone can miss it ([[conformational kinetics]]).


Claims that use this term: [[Static structures alone cannot explain many biological phenomena; dynamics underlie disease mechanisms and drug response.]], [[Allosteric communication remains poorly characterised in most biological systems.]], [[Linking fast molecular motions to slow pathological processes requires conformational dynamics that static structures cannot capture.]], [[EGFR T790M inhibitor resistance can arise through active-state stabilisation without steric obstruction of the inhibitor-binding site.]].
