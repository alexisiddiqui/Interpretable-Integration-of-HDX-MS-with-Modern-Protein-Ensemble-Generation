## Definition

Protein dynamics are the internal motions and conformational changes of a protein, from fast local fluctuations to slow transitions between distinct states. The rigid view of proteins has given way to a dynamic one in which these motions are essential to function. They span a broad range of timescales, and functionally important changes such as allosteric activation can be driven by millisecond motions without large structural rearrangements. Many of these processes are slower than classical all-atom MD can reach.

## Why it matters here

Section 1.1 argues that static structures miss the dynamics behind function, disease and drug response. The rest of the thesis is about measuring those dynamics ([[HDX-MS]]) and estimating them ([[molecular dynamics]] and learned generators), then reconciling the two as a [[structural ensemble]].

Claims that use this term: [[Static structures alone cannot explain many biological phenomena; dynamics underlie disease mechanisms and drug response.]], [[Mounting evidence suggests that understanding dynamics is the key to novel therapeutic strategies.]], [[There is a persistent, critical gap in our ability to predict and understand protein dynamics.]], [[Tools have been dominated by methods that freeze proteins in time, hiding links between structure, dynamics, function and disease.]], [[Tools to estimate protein dynamics are decades old, but public datasets hold only thousands to tens of thousands of entries.]], [[The conformational dynamics underlying pathogenic aggregation are central to understanding these diseases.]], [[Linking fast molecular motions to slow pathological processes requires conformational dynamics that static structures cannot capture.]], [[Rate-limiting conformational changes take microseconds to milliseconds, beyond the reach of traditional structural methods.]], [[Different methods can measure protein dynamics, depending on the system, timescale and experimental conditions.]].
