## Definition

A population shift is a change in the relative populations of conformational states that a protein already samples, rather than the creation of a new structure. A mutation, ligand or modification tilts the equilibrium. All the oncogenic EGFR mutants, for example, shift the kinase domain toward its active conformation, and such equilibrium changes are linked to its oncogenic potential. In conformational selection, a binding partner stabilises a pre-existing state. Because conformational ensembles are highly tunable, these shifts can matter for function and drug design.

## Why it matters here

A population shift can leave the dominant structure, and even the active site, almost unchanged, so a single structure cannot show it. A weighted [[structural ensemble]] can show it. Estimating those weights against [[HDX-MS]] by [[ensemble reweighting]] is how the thesis quantifies populations. The shift itself is a change in the [[conformational free-energy landscape]]. When a mutation causes the shift and the result is pathological, it is a [[dynamic disease mechanism]]. That note covers both directions (destabilising the functional state, or over-stabilising the wrong one) and how the environment shifts populations.

A population shift is a statement about occupied states. It says which states gain or lose weight, not the order or rate in which the protein moves between them ([[conformational kinetics]]).
