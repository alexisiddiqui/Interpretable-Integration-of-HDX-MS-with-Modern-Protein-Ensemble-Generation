## Definition

X-ray crystallography determines a protein's structure from a crystal. A monochromatic X-ray beam interacting with the ordered, periodic crystal generates a diffraction pattern, an electron density map is computed from it, and the atomic structure is built into the map. It is the most common tool for determining the structure of drug targets, alongside [[NMR]] and, more recently, [[cryo-EM]]. A crystal structure is a time-averaged snapshot. The distinct crystal structures of PKA, for instance, are now read as time-averaged conformations of the [[structural ensemble]] that governs its function.

## Why it matters here

In Section 1.1, crystallography stands for the static paradigm. It resolves stable states at atomic resolution but misses [[population shift|population shifts]] and [[high-energy intermediate|intermediates]] that never crystallise. [[HDX-MS]] is introduced as orthogonal to it.

Claims that use this term: [[Structural biology has made tremendous strides in solving static protein structures by X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM and NMR.]], [[The fundamental limitations of these methods restrict our understanding to what can be measured experimentally.]], [[Cancer-causing p53 mutants have crystal structures nearly identical to wild type but very different DNA binding and pathologies.]], [[These neurodegenerative diseases involve transient oligomeric intermediates that never crystallize.]], [[High-energy intermediates with enhanced conformational heterogeneity precede aggregation but resist crystallization.]], [[Combining MD simulations with NMR chemical shifts has revealed misfolding intermediates invisible to crystallography.]], [[Orthogonal to X-ray, cryo-EM and NMR, HDX-MS probes protein dynamics in solution under diverse conditions.]], [[NMR relaxation showed the central helix of calmodulin is flexible in solution, though it appears rigid in crystal structures.]].
